Poole, explained simply · read this first, then the terms page

💡 Oxygen loses pressure at every step from the air to your cells, and it only moves because there is always a lower pressure waiting downstream.
🖼️ Picture it: Water running downhill: air (159) → windpipe (149) → alveoli (100) → blood leaving lung (100) → veins (40) → mitochondria (~1–5).

The plain-English version

  • Dalton: the O₂ pressure = total air pressure × 21%. At altitude the 21% doesn't change; the total pressure drops, so O₂ pressure drops.
  • Why alveoli have only ~100 (not 159): 1) air gets moistened in the airways (water vapor takes up space) → 149; 2) it mixes with CO₂ in the alveoli; 3) you take out more O₂ than you put back CO₂ (R = 0.8). ⭐ He has asked this on exams.
  • Alveolar gas equation: just a recipe for #2 and #3: alveolar O₂ = 149 − (CO₂ ÷ 0.8) ≈ 100.
  • Henry: O₂ barely dissolves in blood (that's why we need hemoglobin). CO₂ dissolves ~20× better.
  • So CO₂ needs only a tiny push (46 → 40 = 6) while O₂ needs a big push (100 → 40 = 60).
  • Boyle: squeeze a gas → pressure up. Diaphragm pulls down → chest gets bigger → pressure drops below air → air rushes in.
  • Healthy lung loads O₂ in ~0.25 s out of ~0.75 s → lots of spare time.
  • Thick barrier (fibrosis, proteinosis): fine at rest, but exercise speeds blood up and there isn't enough time → the animal gets low O₂ when it runs → "Fluffy won't run."
  • Mild lung disease → O₂ drops first, CO₂ stays normal because the body breathes more to fix CO₂ (and CO₂ diffuses easily).
