🔌 LO1 · Spinal nerve components & sympathetic trunk

Order: spinal cord → … → branches (slides 8, 10)?Dorsal + ventral rootlets → dorsal root (with dorsal root ganglion) + ventral root → spinal nerve → dorsal branch, ventral branch, communicating branch (to sympathetic trunk)
Dorsal root: function and cell body location (slide 8)?Sensory only; cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglion; includes both somatic and visceral afferents
Ventral root: function and cell body location (slide 8)?Motor only; cell bodies in the spinal cord
Branches of the spinal nerve (slides 8, 10)?Dorsal branch (ramus), ventral branch (ramus), communicating branch (ramus communicans) to the sympathetic trunk
Dorsal branch and ventral branch are sensory, motor, or mixed (slide 8)?Mixed nerves (figure labels 'mixed nerve')
Fiber types in every spinal nerve's branches (slide 8)?General somatic afferent (GSA), general visceral afferent (GVA), general visceral efferent (GVE), general somatic efferent (GSE)
Do spinal nerves communicate with the sympathetic trunk at every level (slide 8)?Yes — at EVERY level
Where are preganglionic sympathetic cell bodies vs where are postganglionic sympathetic fibers (slide 8)?Preganglionic cell bodies only in thoracolumbar segments (figure: T1–L4(5)); but every spinal nerve carries postganglionic sympathetic fibers
Sympathetic trunk ganglia are also called… (slide 8)Paravertebral ganglia
Where do the roots join to form the spinal nerve (slide 10)?Just distal to the dorsal root ganglion, often at the intervertebral foramen
Labels on the 3-D spinal nerve figure (slide 10)?White matter, gray matter, dorsal and ventral rootlets, dorsal root, ventral root, dorsal root ganglion, dorsal ramus, ventral ramus, spinal nerve, rami communicantes, sympathetic trunk ganglion
Direction of impulses in communicating branches (slide 11)?Both from and toward the spinal nerve; the branches may be fused
White vs gray communicating branch (slide 11, Fig 08-53 #5–6)?White = preganglionic · gray = postganglionic; often fused
Where does a sympathetic fiber synapse (slide 11)?Depends on destination — as close as possible to the destination (chain ganglion vs prevertebral ganglion)
Prevertebral ganglia named on slide 11?Celiac, cranial mesenteric, or caudal mesenteric ganglion — reached via splanchnic nerves
Fig 08-53 numbers 1–9 (slide 11)?1 dorsal root ganglion · 2 ventral root · 3 dorsal branch · 4 ventral branch · 5/6 white/gray communicating branches · 7 sympathetic trunk with ganglia · 8 prevertebral ganglion · 9 gut
Fig 08-53 letters a–f (slide 11)?a somatic afferent · b visceral afferent · c somatic efferent · d visceral efferent (preganglionic sympathetic) · e postganglionic sympathetic to peripheral structures · f postganglionic sympathetic to abdominal organs
Fig 08-53 line code (slide 11)?Visceral = black, somatic = red; afferent = interrupted lines, efferent = solid; postganglionic sympathetic = alternating black/stippled
Human spinal nerve figure labels (slide 41)?Anterior (ventral) root, posterior (dorsal) root, paravertebral ganglion, spinal nerve, sympathetic trunk, grey ramus, white ramus, pia mater, arachnoid mater, dura mater, transverse process, intervertebral foramen, body of vertebra

Visceral vs somatic efferent cell bodies (slide 9)?Visceral efferent = lateral gray horn, in specific regions of the cord only · somatic efferent = ventral gray horn

Midline landmarks of the cord, dorsal vs ventral (slides 9, 14)?Dorsal median sulcus (dorsal) · ventral median fissure (ventral)
What does white matter contain and how is it organized (slide 13)?Axons to/from cord and brain or to another cord region; organized as tracts (not visible) within the funiculi

Fig 08-18B tracts 3–11 (slide 14)?3 lateral corticospinal · 4 rubrospinal · 5 dorsal spinocerebellar · 6 ventral spinocerebellar · 7 spino-olivary and olivospinal · 8 propriospinal system (fasciculi proprii) · 9 spinothalamic · 10 ventral corticospinal · 11 vestibulospinal
Where do the pyramidal tracts cross (slide 14)?At the junction of the brain and spinal cord (curved dotted arrows)

⚡ LO3 · Spinal reflexes

Spinal reflexes are… (slide 21)Local and involuntary (not under conscious control in most cases)
Functions of reflexes (slide 21)?Regulate muscle activity and tension, smooth coordinated movement, maintain posture, rapid protective response
Monosynaptic patellar reflex chain (Fig 08-05, slide 21)?1 tendon stretched → 2 muscle spindles → 3 afferent neuron → spinal cord → 4 efferent neuron → 5 axon → 6 quadriceps contracts
What else happens in the monosynaptic example besides contraction (slide 21)?Inhibition of the opposing muscles (reciprocal inhibition, slide 23)
How common are monosynaptic reflexes (slide 21)?Uncommon
Most common reflex type (slide 22)?Reflex involving an interneuron (polysynaptic)
Interneuron reflex chain (Fig 08-06, slide 22)?1 skin receptor → 2 afferent neuron → 3 synapse on interneuron → 4 synapse at efferent neuron → 5 muscle (6 = spinal cord)
Hot-stove example result (slide 22)?Biceps brachii and other flexors contract, extensors inhibited — before you are aware
Does a spinal reflex need the brain (slide 23)?No — happens locally; collateral branches inform the brain
Why reflexes are clinically useful (slide 23)?They test the integrity of a section of spinal cord; works whether or not the animal is aware

🌙 LO6 · Autonomic nervous system review

Why does every spinal nerve get sympathetic postganglionic fibers (slide 46)?Skin is primarily innervated by sympathetic fibers
Which nerves carry parasympathetic fibers (slides 46, 50)?4 cranial nerves (III, VII, IX, X) and sacral spinal nerves
Parasympathetics in skin (slide 46)?Small number, especially in the face; regulate blood flow and perspiration
Autonomic targets in the head (slide 47)?Smooth muscle: pupil, ciliary body, piloerector, vascular · glands: salivary, lacrimal, nasal
Fig 08-71 numbers 1–5 (slide 47)?1–4 parasympathetic nuclei of III (oculomotor), VII (facial), IX (glossopharyngeal), X (vagus) · 5 cranial cervical ganglion (sympathetic trunk)
Parasympathetic ganglia of the head (Fig 08-71, slide 47)?6 ciliary · 7 pterygopalatine · 8 mandibular · 9 otic (very small)
Nerves 10–14 on Fig 08-71 (slide 47)?10 short ciliary nerves · 11 greater petrosal · 12 deep petrosal · 13 chorda tympani · 14 tympanic plexus/short petrosal nerve
Cervical sympathetic chain, cranial → caudal (slide 48)?Cranial cervical ganglion ← vagosympathetic trunk ← middle cervical ganglion ← cervicothoracic ganglion ← sympathetic chain (thorax, rami communicantes)
Other labels on the vagosympathetic schematic (slide 48)?Vertebral nerve (from cervicothoracic ganglion), rami communicantes, vagus nerve, sympathetic chain; red circle = loop around the subclavian artery (ansa subclavia — outside knowledge, confirm)
Most cranial sympathetic ganglion (slide 49)?Cranial cervical ganglion
Which sympathetics synapse in the cranial cervical ganglion (slide 49)?All sympathetics to the head (and some to the neck)
How do postganglionic sympathetics reach head targets (slide 49)?Follow vessels or join cranial or spinal nerves
Color code in the Nickel figure (slides 49–50)?Sympathetic = blue · parasympathetic = red
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic: pupil, heart, bronchioles (slide 51)?Pupil dilation vs constriction · heart ↑ rate and force vs ↓ rate · bronchioles dilation vs constriction
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic: gut wall, gut sphincter (slide 51)?↓ motility vs ↑ motility · sphincter constrict vs relax
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic: bladder detrusor, bladder-urethra sphincter (slide 51)?Detrusor relax vs contract · sphincter constrict vs relax
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic: penis, gallbladder (slide 51)?Ejaculation vs erection · gallbladder relax vs contract
Sympathetic vs parasympathetic: salivary, nasal and lacrimal glands (slide 51)?Concentrated viscous saliva vs abundant watery saliva · vasoconstriction vs abundant secretion
Sympathetic-only effects (slide 51)?Liver glucose production · kidney renin secretion · muscle vessels dilation · cutaneous and visceral vessels constriction · piloerector contraction · sweat glands (cholinergic) secretion · adipose lipolysis · adrenal medulla epinephrine secretion

👁️ LO7 · Horner's syndrome

Sympathetic pathway to the eye, 3 neurons (slides 53–54)?Hypothalamus (upper motor neuron, tract in the lateral funiculus) → preganglionic from T1 cord via communicating branch → cervicothoracic + middle cervical ganglia (no synapse) → cervical sympathetic trunk → cranial cervical ganglion (synapse) → postganglionic to iris dilator
Where do upper motor neurons for sympathetic output originate and go (slide 53)?Hypothalamus → thoracolumbar spinal cord (not shown)
Which nerves carry preganglionic sympathetics for the head to the trunk (slide 53)?Brachial plexus nerves at the cervicothoracic junction (circled, ~T1)
Red / yellow / green pathways on slide 54?Red = hypothalamic higher control (lateral funiculus) · yellow = preganglionic to cranial cervical ganglion · green = postganglionic route to the dilator of the eye
Horner's syndrome definition (slide 55)?Disruption anywhere along the cervical sympathetic pathway from the hypothalamus to the eye
Lesion sites that cause Horner's (slide 55)?Upper motor neurons, roots of the first thoracic nerves, communicating branch, cervicothoracic and/or middle cervical ganglion, cervical sympathetic trunk, cranial cervical ganglion
Lesion types (slide 55)?Central or peripheral; peripheral may be preganglionic or postganglionic
Four signs of Horner's (slide 56)?Ptosis (drooping lid), miosis (constricted pupil, less obvious in bright light), enophthalmos, protrusion of the third eyelid — affected side
Cord level of preganglionic sympathetics for the eye (slide 57)?T1 (maybe T2 as well) — see ⚠️ flag
T1 nerve avulsion (red X, slide 57) → Horner's?YES — injury proximal to the communicating branch interrupts sympathetics to the eye on that side
Lesion of T1 ventral branch distal to the communicating branch (yellow X, slide 57) → Horner's?NO — sympathetic fibers have already left via the communicating branch
Postganglionic route into the orbit (slide 58)?Enter calvarium → ventral to trigeminal ganglion → exit with the ophthalmic nerve (trigeminal) through the orbital fissure → nasociliary nerve → long ciliary nerve
What does the long ciliary nerve supply (slide 58)?Iris dilator muscle and blood vessels of the uveal tract
Other sympathetic smooth-muscle targets around the eye (slide 58)?Smooth muscles of the periorbita and the eyelids (upper eyelid smooth muscle with levator palpebrae superioris)
Mechanism of enophthalmos (slide 59)?Circular periorbital smooth muscle relaxes (loss of sympathetic input) → retractor bulbi unopposed → globe retracted
Third eyelid protrusion: dog vs cat (slide 60)?Dog = passive, secondary to enophthalmos · cat = also active component (sympathetic smooth muscle in third eyelid); 2nd most common sign in cats
Ptosis mechanism (slide 60)?Loss of sympathetic tone in eyelid smooth muscle (some argue secondary to enophthalmos — see ⚠️ flag)
Vascular sign of Horner's (slide 61)?Ipsilateral peripheral vasodilation — warm pinna, hyperemia of nasal planum and/or conjunctiva
