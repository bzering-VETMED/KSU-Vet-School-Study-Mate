⚠️ Precision traps

  • Efficacy = Emax (how big the maximum is); potency = EC50 (how much drug is needed). Do not swap them.
  • More potent = LOWER EC50; less potent = HIGHER EC50. Lower number wins.
  • Competitive antagonist raises EC50 (curve shifts right, Emax kept); non-competitive antagonist lowers Emax.
  • Competitive antagonism is overcome by more agonist because agonist competes for binding sites; it does not kick the antagonist out.
  • Desensitization = short term (milliseconds–minutes); tolerance = long term (days–years).
  • Partial agonist = less than maximal response (can antagonize stronger agonists); super agonist = greater than endogenous full agonist; inverse agonist = opposite effect, needs basal activity.
  • Inverse agonist ≠ antagonist: an antagonist blocks an agonist; an inverse agonist produces the opposite effect on its own (needs basal signaling).
  • Reversible = measurable koff; irreversible = no measurable koff. An irreversible antagonist looks non-competitive regardless of where it binds.
  • Naloxone (competitive, opioid receptor) vs ketamine (non-competitive, NMDA receptor). Buprenorphine = partial agonist, not antagonist.
  • Adenylyl cyclase (enzyme, -ase) makes cAMP from ATP; phosphorylase kinase activates glycogen phosphorylase (kinase comes first).

Signaling dynamicsS1How a cell gets the right amount of signal: amplify it, measure it, and modify it with agonists or antagonists.
PheochromocytomaS2Adrenal tumor that secretes catecholamines (norepinephrine/epinephrine); surgery risks hemodynamic instability from massive release.
Hemodynamic instabilityS2Dangerous blood pressure/heart swings during pheochromocytoma surgery caused by surges of norepinephrine and epinephrine from the tumor.
AmplificationS3A signal going from a few molecules to a huge response; enabled by second messengers.
Second messengerS4Intracellular signal made after the primary signal; allows multiplication (amplification), integration, and modulation of the signal.
Direct effect (no second messenger)S5Primary signal acts directly: 1:1 signal-to-effect ratio, limited by signal concentration, cannot integrate other signals.
1 : >>1 signal-to-effect ratioS5With second messengers, one primary signal produces far more than one unit of effect.
IntegrationS5–S6Second-messenger pathways accept 'other signals' at multiple steps (PKA, phosphorylase kinase, glycogen phosphorylase), combining inputs into one output.
Stimulatory G protein (Gs)S6G protein activated by the epinephrine/glucagon receptor; swaps guanosine diphosphate (GDP) for guanosine triphosphate (GTP), then activates adenylyl cyclase.
Adenylyl cyclaseS6Enzyme activated by Gs; converts adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP).
Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)S6Second messenger made by adenylyl cyclase; activates protein kinase A.
Protein kinase A (PKA)S6cAMP-activated kinase; phosphorylates and activates phosphorylase kinase in the liver cell cascade.
Phosphorylase kinaseS6Kinase phosphorylated by PKA; phosphorylates and activates glycogen phosphorylase.
Glycogen phosphorylaseS6Enzyme activated by phosphorylase kinase; breaks glycogen into glucose-1-phosphate.
Glucose-1-phosphateS6End product of the liver Gs cascade; released from glycogen by glycogen phosphorylase.
~100,000,000-fold amplificationS6Total amplification from very-low-concentration epinephrine/glucagon to glucose-1-phosphate in a liver cell.
Receptor desensitizationS7Receptors respond to changes in signal concentration, not absolute level; sustained agonist gives a decreasing response over time.
Mu-opioid receptor (MOR) toleranceS7Chronic opioid users need higher and higher doses for the same effect.
Long-acting beta-2 (β2) adrenergic agonistS7Asthma drug that relaxes airway smooth muscle; chronic use can cause receptor desensitization.
Desensitization (short term)S7Short-term loss of response: milliseconds to minutes.
Tolerance (long term)S7Long-term loss of response: days to years.
Disabled signalingS8Desensitization method: a kinase phosphorylates the receptor and arrestin binds, blocking signaling.
ArrestinS8–S9Protein that binds the phosphorylated receptor; blocks signaling and accompanies the receptor during internalization.
Membrane cyclingS8Desensitization method: phosphorylated, arrestin-bound receptor is internalized in a vesicle, then degraded or recycled to the surface.
Reduced expressionS8Desensitization method: the cell expresses fewer receptors (less gene expression), so fewer reach the surface.
Desensitization kinetics timelineS9S9 figure: G-protein signal (<~1 s) → phosphorylation → arrestin binding → endocytosis (~10 min–1 hr); resensitization or recycling afterward.
G protein-coupled receptor kinase (GRK)S9Kinase on the S9 timeline that phosphorylates the agonist-bound receptor within seconds (expansion of GRK is OUTSIDE — confirm).
Beta-arrestin-2 (βarr-2)S9Arrestin shown on S9 binding the phosphorylated receptor (~seconds to minutes) before endocytosis.
Rapid (surface) resensitization vs recyclingS9S9 recovery routes: phosphorylated surface receptor resensitizes quickly; endocytosed receptor returns by recycling.
Michaelis-Menten equationS10–S11Effect = Emax × [ligand] / (EC50 + [ligand]); describes magnitude of a ligand/receptor effect.
Maximal effect (Emax)S11The maximal effect a ligand produces; plateau of the dose-response curve.
Half-maximal effective concentration (EC50)S11Ligand concentration (dose) that produces ½ Emax.
EfficacyS10, S12Activation of a receptor by a ligand; measured as Emax. Higher Emax = more efficacious.
PotencyS10, S13Interaction/binding strength of ligand to receptor; measured as EC50. Lower EC50 = more potent.
Semi-logarithmic (semi-log) plotS10, S14Effect vs log [ligand]; turns the hyperbola into an S-curve. Most common display of ligand-receptor interactions.
AgonistS16, S18Ligand that binds a receptor and initiates a physiological response; has measurable potency and efficacy.
Full agonistS18Activates maximal response equal to the endogenous ligand.
Partial agonistS18–S19Activates LESS than maximal response (less efficacious); can act as an antagonist against more efficacious agonists.
Super agonistS18Causes a response GREATER than the endogenous full agonist.
Inverse agonistS18Causes the opposite effect of an agonist; requires receptors with basal signaling activity without agonist.
MorphineS19, S22Agonist at the mu-opioid receptor; residence time in the low-minute range (koff ~0.01/s).
BuprenorphineS19Partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor.
GoserelinS19Super agonist at the luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone receptor (LHRHR); downregulates LHRHR and sex hormones in prostate/breast cancer.
Ro15-4513S19Inverse agonist at the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor; developed to counteract alcohol poisoning.
GABA inverse agonist effectS18Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) inverse agonists can cause anxiety.
AntagonistS16Ligand that both binds the receptor and prevents its activation by an agonist.
Competitive antagonistS17, S20–S21Binds the agonist binding site, prevents agonist binding; raises EC50; overcome by more agonist.
Non-competitive antagonistS17, S20–S21Binds an allosteric site away from the agonist site; inhibits response; lowers Emax.
Allosteric siteS17, S20Receptor site other than the agonist binding site; where non-competitive antagonists bind.
Lock-and-key modelS17Agonist = key that starts car; competitive antagonist = key blank; non-competitive antagonist = car boot.
NaloxoneS21Competitive antagonist at opioid receptors (raises EC50).
KetamineS21Non-competitive antagonist at the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor (lowers Emax).
k-1 (off rate) of antagonistS21Ability to overcome a competitive antagonist partially depends on its off rate.
Off rate (koff)S22Dissociation rate of a ligand from the receptor, per second; sets residence time.
Residence timeS22How long a ligand stays bound to the receptor; inverse of how fast it comes off.
ReversibleS22Measurable koff: the ligand comes back off.
IrreversibleS22No measurable koff: does not come off; often covalently bound to the receptor.
Practically irreversibleS22Off rate so low that physiological functions cannot occur.
Irreversible antagonist looks non-competitiveS22Actual or practical irreversible antagonist lowers Emax regardless of binding site; agonist cannot compete for sites.
PhenoxybenzamineS24Irreversible, non-competitive alpha-adrenergic inhibitor used preoperatively for pheochromocytoma resection.
Low-dose phenoxybenzamine paradoxS24; LC30Tolerable doses shift the curve right instead of depressing max; spare alpha-adrenoceptors are not depleted (answered LC30).
