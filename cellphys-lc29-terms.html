⚠️ Precision traps

  • Efficacy = Emax (how big the maximum is); potency = EC50 (how much drug is needed). Do not swap them.
  • More potent = LOWER EC50; less potent = HIGHER EC50. Lower number wins.
  • Competitive antagonist raises EC50 (curve shifts right, Emax kept); non-competitive antagonist lowers Emax.
  • Competitive antagonism is overcome by more agonist because agonist competes for binding sites; it does not kick the antagonist out.
  • Desensitization = short term (milliseconds–minutes); tolerance = long term (days–years).
  • Inverse agonist ≠ antagonist: an antagonist blocks an agonist; an inverse agonist produces the opposite effect on its own (needs basal signaling).
  • Reversible = measurable koff; irreversible = no measurable koff. An irreversible antagonist looks non-competitive regardless of where it binds.
  • Naloxone (competitive, opioid receptor) vs ketamine (non-competitive, NMDA receptor). Buprenorphine = partial agonist (but can act as a competitive antagonist vs morphine).
  • Adenylyl cyclase (enzyme, -ase) makes cAMP from ATP; phosphorylase kinase activates glycogen phosphorylase (kinase comes first).
  • ★ Second messengers: Gs → cAMP ↑; Gi → NOT cAMP (↓); Gq → IP3 and/or DAG. Steroid receptors have NO second messenger (receptor = transcription factor) (transcript).
  • ★ Shift directions: more efficacious = curve shifts UP (higher Emax); more potent = shifts LEFT (lower EC50). Less potent = right; less efficacious = down (transcript, S12–S14).
  • ★ EC50 units = CONCENTRATION (not % effect, not time). Read it by tracing from ½ Emax down to the x-axis (transcript, S14).
  • Ligand ≠ agonist: ligand just binds (agnostic); agonist ACTIVATES. Every agonist is a ligand, not vice versa (transcript).
  • Desensitization mechanisms by speed: receptor phosphorylation and arrestin = seconds–minutes; internalization / reduced expression = hours–days (transcript).
  • Emax is never actually reached (requires infinite agonist). Receptors respond to changes, not absolute concentration (transcript, S7, S11).
  • ★ Antagonist has NO potency or efficacy of its own (no effect alone). It changes the agonist's efficacy and/or potency. Agonist = has both (transcript).
  • ★ Partial agonist = lower Emax, yet it acts as a competitive antagonist against a full agonist (buprenorphine vs morphine). Super = higher Emax; inverse = below baseline (transcript, S18–S19).
  • Competitive = surmountable (↑ EC50, shift right, Emax same). Non-competitive = insurmountable (↓ Emax, EC50 same). Don't flip which parameter moves (transcript, S21).
  • koff depends only on bond strength (and temperature), not on agonist concentration. Excess agonist wins only when the antagonist comes off on its own (transcript).
  • ★ Practically irreversible = koff is measurable but much longer than the receptor's activity window. Not "no koff" (that is true irreversible) (transcript, S22).
  • Goserelin (super agonist, LHRH/GnRH) vs Ro15-4513 (inverse agonist, GABA; designed as an antagonist but made people anxious) (transcript, S19).

Signaling dynamicsS1How a cell gets the right amount of signal: amplify it, measure it, and modify it with agonists or antagonists.
Plug-and-play pathwaystranscript★ Dr. Schultz's classic pathways (steroid, Gs/Gi/Gq, tyrosine kinase, guanylyl cyclase): "really good if you ... could walk through those pathways." Pharmacology expects it.
Gradient signalingS1; transcriptPathways are GRADIENTS, not on/off: more or less signal gives more or less effect.
PheochromocytomaS2; transcriptAdrenal tumor secreting catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine, some dopamine) → extreme sympathetic signal everywhere; poking it in surgery releases more.
CatecholaminesS2; transcriptEpinephrine, norepinephrine, and (to some degree) dopamine.
Hemodynamic instabilityS2; transcriptBlood pressure swinging wildly ("sometimes 800/600, sometimes 0/0") from catecholamine surges; both extremes inconsistent with life.
AmplificationS3; transcriptGoing from a very low signal concentration to a relatively large cell effect, via second messenger steps.
Second messengerS4; transcript★ Intracellular signal after the primary signal; allows AMPLIFICATION, MODULATION, and INTEGRATION. Gs → cAMP; Gi → ↓ cAMP; Gq → IP3 and/or DAG.
Gs → cAMP; Gi → ↓ cAMP; Gq → IP3/DAGS4; transcript★ "Memorize in your sleep": Gs second messenger = cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP); Gi = NOT cAMP (↓); Gq = inositol trisphosphate (IP3) and/or diacylglycerol (DAG).
Steroid receptor: no second messengertranscriptSteroid hormone receptor itself goes to the nucleus as a transcription factor; example of signaling without second messengers.
ModulationS4–S5; transcriptSecond-messenger steps (G proteins, kinases, phosphatases) let the cell turn the signal up or down.
Direct effect (no second messenger)S5; transcriptPrimary signal acts directly: 1:1 signal-to-effect, limited by receptor number and signal concentration, cannot integrate; "literally an on-off system."
1 : >>1 signal-to-effect ratioS5With second messengers, one primary signal produces far more than one unit of effect.
IntegrationS5–S6; transcriptOther signals merge into the same second-messenger pathway: two Gs signals synergize, a Gi signal counteracts; one decision point.
Synergy (two Gs signals)S6; transcriptGlucagon + epinephrine together both raise cAMP → liver breaks down glycogen "really, really fast."
Gi counteracts GsS6; transcriptA Gi signal on the same cell counteracts Gs → less effect than Gs alone.
Liver glycogen inputsS6; transcriptGlucagon, epinephrine, insulin, glucocorticoids, growth hormone, AMP:ATP status, citrate — integrated into one glycogen phosphorylase decision.
Stimulatory G protein (Gs)S6; transcriptLiver glucagon and epinephrine receptors are both Gs-coupled. Receptor acts as guanine nucleotide exchange factor: GDP out, GTP in; αs leaves βγ → adenylyl cyclase.
Adenylyl cyclaseS6Enzyme activated by Gs; converts adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP).
Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)S6Second messenger made by adenylyl cyclase; activates protein kinase A.
Protein kinase A (PKA)S6cAMP-activated kinase; phosphorylates and activates phosphorylase kinase in the liver cell cascade.
Phosphorylase kinaseS6Kinase phosphorylated by PKA; phosphorylates and activates glycogen phosphorylase.
Glycogen phosphorylaseS6Enzyme activated by phosphorylase kinase; breaks glycogen into glucose-1-phosphate.
Glucose-1-phosphateS6End product of the liver Gs cascade; released from glycogen by glycogen phosphorylase.
Why each step multipliesS6; transcriptEach activated molecule keeps acting while active: receptor (while agonist bound), Gαs (while GTP bound), PKA (while cAMP bound), each kinase (while phosphorylated).
~100,000,000-fold amplificationS6Total amplification from very-low-concentration epinephrine/glucagon to glucose-1-phosphate in a liver cell.
Receptor desensitizationS7; transcript★ Receptors respond to CHANGES in signal concentration, not absolute concentration; sustained signal → less and less effect.
Mu-opioid receptor (MOR) toleranceS7; transcriptChronic opioid users (medical or recreational) need more and more for the same effect → can lead to overdose as other pathways overactivate.
Long-acting beta-2 (β2) adrenergic agonistS7; transcriptAirway smooth muscle relaxant (e.g. albuterol); daily use desensitizes, so daily asthma maintenance uses a glucocorticoid instead.
Desensitization (short term)S7; transcriptShort-term loss of response (slide: milliseconds–minutes). Basel: some systems desensitize within milliseconds–seconds.
Tolerance (long term)S7; transcriptLong-term loss of response (slide: days–years; Basel: hours to days and longer).
Disabled signalingS8; transcriptFast desensitization: a kinase PHOSPHORYLATES the receptor (seconds–minutes) and arrestin binds, blocking signaling.
ArrestinS8–S9; transcriptProtein that binds phosphorylated receptors and blocks signaling (seconds–minutes); accompanies receptor internalization.
Membrane cyclingS8; transcriptReceptors pulled off the membrane (internalized) into vesicles → degraded or recycled. Basel groups it with hours–days changes.
Reduced expressionS8; transcriptCell makes fewer receptors (hours–days) so it stays sensitive to changes, not absolute signal.
Insulin receptor desensitizationtranscriptLong-term deactivation of insulin receptors can lead toward type 2 diabetes. Basel unsure of exact kinetics (see flag).
Desensitization kinetics timelineS9S9 figure: G-protein signal (<~1 s) → phosphorylation → arrestin binding → endocytosis (~10 min–1 hr); resensitization or recycling afterward.
G protein-coupled receptor kinase (GRK)S9Kinase on the S9 timeline that phosphorylates the agonist-bound receptor within seconds (expansion of GRK is OUTSIDE — confirm).
Beta-arrestin-2 (βarr-2)S9Arrestin shown on S9 binding the phosphorylated receptor (~seconds to minutes) before endocytosis.
Rapid (surface) resensitization vs recyclingS9S9 recovery routes: phosphorylated surface receptor resensitizes quickly; endocytosed receptor returns by recycling.
Michaelis-Menten equationS10–S11; transcriptEffect = Emax × [ligand] / (EC50 + [ligand]). Applies to receptor AND enzyme pathways. ★ "I don't need you to do math."
8-receptor thought experimentS10–S11; transcriptLittle agonist + many free receptors → sharp rise in effect; as receptors fill, less effect per added agonist; saturated → no further change.
Kon / KoffS10–S11; transcriptOn/off binding rates (Dr. Schultz). Many free receptors → high Kon; as receptors fill, Kon falls until Koff balances it.
Ligand vs agonistS16; transcriptLigand = anything that binds a receptor (agnostic to effect). Agonist = ligand that ACTIVATES it.
Maximal effect (Emax)S11; transcriptMaximal possible effect (all receptors maximally active); only at infinite concentration, so never actually reached. Measure of efficacy.
Half-maximal effective concentration (EC50)S11; transcript★ Concentration giving ½ Emax; tells how sensitive the pathway is to the signal. ALWAYS has concentration units.
EfficacyS10, S12; transcript★ = Emax: how much effect you could get at very high signal. Higher Emax = more efficacious; curve shifts UP.
PotencyS10, S13; transcript★ = EC50: how much signal is needed for the effect. LOWER EC50 = MORE potent; curve shifts LEFT.
Semi-logarithmic (semi-log) plotS10, S14; transcript★ Linear effect vs log [ligand] (each unit = ×10). Sigmoid curve; easy to read Emax and EC50. "Make sure you can read a semi-log plot."
Linear dose-response plotS10–S13; transcriptHyperbola with EC50s clustered in the corner; used only for thought experiments ("last time you'll see this").
AgonistS16, S18; transcriptLigand that binds AND activates the receptor; has measurable potency (EC50) AND efficacy (Emax) of its own.
Antagonist has no potency or efficacy of its ownS16; transcript★ Antagonist binds but causes no effect on its own → no efficacy → no potency. It CHANGES the agonist's efficacy and/or potency.
Endogenous ligandtranscriptThe body's own ligand for a receptor: aldosterone at mineralocorticoid receptor, cortisol at glucocorticoid receptor, endorphins at mu-opioid receptor.
Full agonistS18–S19; transcriptSame efficacy (Emax) as the endogenous ligand; e.g. morphine at the mu-opioid receptor.
Partial agonistS18–S19; transcriptBinds and activates but LOWER Emax than the full agonist, even at very high concentration. ★ "A partial agonist can act as a competitive antagonist against a full agonist."
Super agonistS18–S19; transcriptResponse GREATER than the endogenous full agonist (higher Emax); can overactivate then suppress (downregulate) the receptor.
Inverse agonistS18; transcriptOpposite effect of an agonist: turns baseline (basal) signaling BELOW the set point. Has an effect on its own → NOT an antagonist.
MorphineS19, S22; transcriptFull agonist at the mu-opioid receptor; koff ~0.01/s, sits ~5–10 minutes.
BuprenorphineS19; transcriptPartial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor; pain relief with fewer side effects; used long term in opioid addiction to occupy the site and blunt stronger opioids.
GoserelinS19; transcriptSuper agonist at the luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) receptor (Basel: GnRH receptor) → overactivation then downregulation → blocks fertility or treats sex-hormone-responsive cancers.
Ro15-4513S19; transcriptInverse agonist at the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor. Designed as a GABA antagonist for alcoholism; instead made people hyper-anxious and jittery.
GABA receptors are inhibitoryS18; transcriptGamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors inhibit neurons; alcohol activates them (relaxed); GABA inverse agonists cause anxiety. "Dr. Schultz will very much want you to know."
AntagonistS16; transcriptLigand that binds the receptor and prevents its activation by an agonist; no effect on its own.
Competitive antagonistS17, S20–S21; transcript★ Binds the agonist binding site. Surmountable: excess agonist out-competes it → EC50 ↑ (less potent, curve shifts RIGHT), Emax unchanged.
Non-competitive antagonistS17, S20–S21; transcript★ Binds a different (allosteric) site; agonist concentration irrelevant → insurmountable → Emax ↓, potency (EC50) unchanged.
Surmountable vs insurmountableS21; transcriptCompetitive = overcome by raising agonist concentration; non-competitive = "we can never overcome" no matter how much agonist.
Allosteric siteS17, S20; transcriptReceptor site other than the agonist binding site; where non-competitive antagonists bind.
Lock-and-key modelS17; transcriptAgonist = car key; competitive antagonist = key BLANK in the ignition (blocks the site); non-competitive = K-State parking BOOT (binds elsewhere, car can't go).
NaloxoneS21; transcriptCompetitive antagonist at the mu-opioid receptor; given intranasally for overdose; patient wakes in instant withdrawal and may attack you.
KetamineS21; transcriptNon-competitive antagonist at the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor (lowers Emax).
k-1 (off rate) of antagonistS21; transcript★ Overcoming a competitive antagonist depends on its koff: fast koff = easy to out-compete; slow koff = long window where agonist amount doesn't matter.
Off rate (koff)S22; transcriptDissociation rate (per second). Depends only on bond strength (and temperature) — agonist concentration cannot change it or "kick off" the antagonist.
Residence timeS22; transcriptHow long a ligand stays bound; inverse of koff. Acetylcholine ~1 ms or less; morphine minutes.
ReversibleS22; transcriptMeasurable koff: the ligand comes back off.
IrreversibleS22; transcriptNo measurable koff: never comes off; usually a COVALENT bond (non-covalent bonds ≈ energy available, so they come apart).
Practically irreversibleS22; transcript★ Measurable koff, but far longer than the receptor's activity window (e.g. a 3-second block of the nicotinic receptor wipes out the millisecond acetylcholine signal).
Irreversible antagonist looks non-competitiveS22; transcript★ "Irreversible (true or practical) antagonists ALWAYS look non-competitive," regardless of binding site: the agonist can never compete.
Nicotinic acetylcholine receptorS22; transcriptSkeletal muscle receptor (non-selective cation channel); acetylcholine koff ~1000/s, ~1 ms residence; whole signal lasts a few ms.
PhenoxybenzamineS24; transcriptIrreversible (→ acts non-competitive) alpha-adrenoceptor antagonist used pre-op for pheochromocytoma; chosen because a catecholamine surge would out-compete a competitive antagonist.
Low-dose phenoxybenzamine paradoxS24; LC30 transcript"But the dog still died": it looked competitive (right shift, Emax kept). Answer (LC30, Fri 11/6): spare alpha-adrenoceptors not depleted; higher dose is toxic.
