🧠 Cranial nerve basics & fiber types (slides 9–11)

How are cranial nerves numbered (slide 9)?Rostral to caudal; the names tell you something about structure or function
Cranial nerves are connected to the brain stem — which are the exceptions (slide 10)?CN I (olfactory) and part of CN XI (accessory)
4 ways cranial nerves differ from spinal nerves (slide 10)?① Some are only motor or only sensory (spinal nerves are all mixed) · ② some carry special sensory information (no spinal nerve does) · ③ only SOME carry autonomic fibers (all spinal nerves do) · ④ some innervate muscles that develop in the branchial (pharyngeal) arches
General somatic afferent (GSA) = (slide 11)?Sensory from the body surface: touch, pressure, heat, noxious stimuli, proprioception
General visceral afferent (GVA) = (slide 11)?Sensory impulses like pain or reflex sensations from internal organs, glands, and blood vessels
General somatic efferent (GSE) = (slide 11)?Motor to skeletal muscle
General visceral efferent (GVE) = (slide 11)?Motor to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands (autonomic)
Which 4 fiber types are in ALL spinal nerves and some cranial nerves (slide 11)?General somatic afferent (GSA), general visceral afferent (GVA), general somatic efferent (GSE), general visceral efferent (GVE)
Special somatic afferent (SSA) = (slide 11)?Vision, balance, hearing — 'How is my BODY placed in the environment?'
Special visceral afferent (SVA) = (slide 11)?Taste and smell — 'visceral' functions
Special visceral efferent (SVE) = (slide 11)?Motor to striated muscle that develops in the pharyngeal arches: muscles of mastication, facial expression, and those innervated by the accessory nerve. Slide notes this classification has little value in real applications
Which fiber types exist ONLY in some cranial nerves, never all in the same nerve (slide 11)?Special somatic afferent (SSA), special visceral afferent (SVA), special visceral efferent (SVE)

👃 CN I · Olfactory nerve (slides 13–14)

Olfactory nerve (I): pathway from nose to brain (slide 13)?Cell bodies in the olfactory epithelium (nasal mucosa) → nonmyelinated axons → foramina of the CRIBRIFORM PLATE of the ETHMOID bone → olfactory bulb
Olfactory nerve (I) function and fiber type (slides 11, 13)?Olfaction (smell) — special visceral afferent (SVA)
Is the olfactory nerve a single nerve (slide 13)?No — numerous small nonmyelinated fibers, distributed to the nasal mucosa
Olfactory nerve origin: brain division (slide 14)?Telencephalon (olfactory bulb → olfactory peduncle → piriform lobe)
Fun fact, slide 13: how does Naegleria fowleri reach the brain?Adheres to the olfactory nerve in the nasal cavity → migrates along it to the olfactory bulb → crosses the cribriform plate into the brain (attracted to chemicals released by the fibers)

👁️ CN II · Optic nerve (slides 15, 20)

Optic nerve (II): pathway (slide 15)?Retina → optic nerve within the periorbita, wrapped in meninges, surrounded by retractor bulbi muscles → OPTIC CANAL → diencephalon
Optic nerve (II) function and fiber type (slides 11, 15)?Vision — special somatic afferent (SSA)
Why is the optic nerve 'technically a tract, not a nerve' (slide 15)?It is an extension of the DIENCEPHALON connecting the retina with the diencephalon
What's around the optic nerve in the orbit (slide 15)?Periorbita, an extension of the meninges, and the retractor bulbi muscles
Why does papilledema (optic disc swelling) indicate increased intracranial pressure (slide 15)?The optic nerve's meningeal sheath is continuous with the brain's meninges
In the orbit, which two nerves are very closely associated (slide 20)?Optic nerve (II) and oculomotor nerve (III)

👁️ CN III · Oculomotor nerve (slides 16–20)

Oculomotor nerve (III): origin and exit (slide 17)?Ventral aspect of the MESENCEPHALON → exits the cranial cavity via the ORBITAL FISSURE
Oculomotor (III) skeletal muscle targets (slide 17)?Many extrinsic ocular muscles: ventral oblique; dorsal, ventral, and medial rectus — plus levator palpebrae superioris
Oculomotor (III) parasympathetic pathway (slide 18)?Preganglionic parasympathetic fibers in III → synapse in the CILIARY GANGLION → postganglionic fibers in the SHORT CILIARY NERVES → ciliary muscle + constrictor pupillae muscle
What other fibers travel in the short ciliary nerves (slide 18)?Sympathetic postganglionic fibers too
Oculomotor (III) dysfunction — 3 signs (slide 19)?Lateral strabismus, ptosis of the upper (superior) eyelid, mydriasis
Why lateral strabismus with a CN III lesion (slide 19)?Lateral rectus (VI) still works but medial rectus (III) doesn't → eye pulled outward (exotropia)
Why ptosis with a CN III lesion (slide 19)?Levator palpebrae superioris isn't working
Why mydriasis with a CN III lesion (slide 19)?Constrictor pupillae isn't working (lost parasympathetic)
Define strabismus, esotropia, exotropia (slide 19)?Strabismus = misalignment of the eyes; esotropia = deviates inward toward the nose; exotropia = deviates outward
Oculomotor (III) fiber types (outside the slide wording)?Slides list motor (somatic, to extraocular muscles) + parasympathetic (to ciliary and constrictor pupillae) — i.e., general somatic efferent (GSE) + general visceral efferent (GVE) (fiber-type labels: outside knowledge — confirm)

🔄 CN IV · Trochlear nerve (slides 16, 21–22)

Trochlear nerve (IV): origin and exit (slides 16, 21)?DORSAL aspect of the MESENCEPHALON → decussates over the midline → exits the cranial cavity via the ORBITAL FISSURE (ventrally)
What is unique about the trochlear nerve's origin (slide 21)?The ONLY cranial nerve to originate from the dorsal aspect of the brain stem
Trochlear (IV) innervates which side's muscle (slide 16)?The OPPOSITE side — it decussates over the midline
Trochlear (IV) function (slide 21)?Motor to the DORSAL OBLIQUE muscle of the eye
Trochlear (IV) dysfunction sign (slide 22)?Extorsion of the eye (dorsal oblique not working)
Define extorsion and why it happens with a IV lesion (slide 22)?Outward rotation where the top of the eyeball twists away from the nose — the ventral oblique works unopposed

😬 CN V · Trigeminal nerve (slides 23–27, 34)

Which cranial nerves originate from the metencephalon (slide 23)?V, VI, VII, VIII (VIII sits right on the junction with the medulla)
What does 'trigeminal' mean (slide 24)?Tri- + geminus = 'born in threes' — it splits into 3 nerves
Where does the trigeminal divide into its 3 nerves (slides 24–25)?As it emerges from the TRIGEMINAL CANAL (in the petrous temporal bone)
V1 ophthalmic nerve: exit (slide 25)?ORBITAL FISSURE
V2 maxillary nerve: exit pathway (slide 25)?ROUND foramen → ALAR CANAL → ROSTRAL ALAR FORAMEN
V3 mandibular nerve: exit (slide 25)?OVAL foramen
Which 4 nerves pass through the orbital fissure (slide 25)?III oculomotor, IV trochlear, V1 ophthalmic, VI abducent
Do the ophthalmic and maxillary nerves share a foramen (slide 26)?No — ophthalmic = orbital fissure; maxillary = round foramen
Ophthalmic nerve (V1) function (slide 26)?Sensory (general somatic afferent, GSA) to and around the eye, especially dorsomedially
Maxillary nerve (V2) function (slide 26)?Sensory (GSA) to and around the upper (superior) dental arcade and lip, plus the lateral canthus area
Mandibular nerve (V3) sensory function (slide 26)?Sensory (GSA) to and around the lower (inferior) dental arcade, lip, tongue, and skin of the cheek
Mandibular nerve (V3) motor function and fiber type (slide 26)?Motor to the muscles of MASTICATION EXCEPT the CAUDAL belly of the digastricus — special visceral efferent (SVE)
How does the mandibular nerve reach the skin of the cheek (slide 26)?The AURICULOTEMPORAL nerve (V3) joins the DORSAL BUCCAL branch — slide says 'of trigeminal'; normally a facial (VII) branch (outside knowledge — confirm; see ⚠️ FLAG)
Trigeminal (V) is sensory/motor/both per the mnemonic (slide 12)?Both ('But')
Slide 27 discussion: cutaneous zone vs autonomous zone?Cutaneous zone = the whole skin area a nerve supplies (overlaps neighbors); autonomous zone = the area supplied ONLY by that nerve, so it's the one you test for that nerve's deficit (outside knowledge — confirm)
Autonomous zones of the head shown on slide 27 (dog figure)?Ophthalmic, maxillary, and mandibular nerve (V) zones, plus facial nerve and dorsal/ventral cutaneous branches of C2–C3
Dissection note: which 2 cranial nerves both have superficial facial branches, and which pair sits in the same area (slide 34)?Trigeminal (V) and facial (VII); their branches run together in places. Auriculotemporal (V) lies in the same area as auriculopalpebral (VII)
L28 cross-check: L28 listed maxillary → major + minor palatine nerves and mandibular → nerve to tensor veli palatini. Does L35 repeat this?Not shown in L35 — no conflict; L35 only gives the 3 divisions, exits, and sensory/motor fields

↔️ CN VI · Abducent nerve (slides 28–29)

Abducent nerve (VI): origin and exit (slides 23, 28)?Metencephalon → exits the cranial cavity via the ORBITAL FISSURE
Abducent (VI) function and fiber type (slide 28)?General somatic efferent (GSE) to the LATERAL RECTUS and RETRACTOR BULBI muscles
Fun fact (slide 28): which species lacks a retractor bulbi?Humans
Abducent (VI) dysfunction (slide 29)?MEDIAL strabismus — the lateral rectus isn't working

🙂 CN VII · Facial nerve (slides 30–34)

Facial nerve (VII): pathway through the skull (slide 30)?Metencephalon → INTERNAL ACOUSTIC MEATUS → FACIAL CANAL → emerges at the STYLOMASTOID FORAMEN
Facial (VII): motor targets (slide 30)?Muscles of facial expression + the CAUDAL belly of the digastricus
Facial (VII) parasympathetic route 1 (slide 30)?Facial → pterygopalatine ganglion → lacrimal, nasal, and palatine glands
Facial (VII) parasympathetic route 2 (slides 30, 58)?Facial → chorda tympani → lingual nerve → mandibular (submandibular) and sublingual salivary glands
Facial (VII) taste (slide 30)?Taste from the ROSTRAL 2/3 of the tongue via the CHORDA TYMPANI joining the lingual nerve (V3)
Facial (VII) is sensory/motor/both (slides 12, 30)?Both ('Brother')
Slide 31 clicker: 'Which side is normal?' — what condition is being shown?Facial nerve paralysis (drooping lip/ear/eyelid on the affected side). The answer letter isn't shown on the slide
Facial nerve clinical significance — 3 points (slide 32)?① Close to the EXTERNAL ACOUSTIC MEATUS (ear disease/surgery risk) · ② branches are superficial and vulnerable · ③ the AURICULOPALPEBRAL nerve crosses the ZYGOMATIC ARCH to the eyelid and ear muscles
Slide 33 discussion: why block the auriculopalpebral nerve?To paralyze eyelid closure (orbicularis oculi) so you can examine/treat the eye — horse eye exam shown (outside knowledge — confirm)
Inner ear infections can affect which cranial nerves (slide 36)?CN VII OR CN VIII

👂 CN VIII · Vestibulocochlear nerve (slides 35–37)

Vestibulocochlear nerve (VIII): pathway (slide 35)?Metencephalon (near facial) → INTERNAL ACOUSTIC MEATUS → ends in the membranous labyrinth of the inner ear — does NOT emerge from the skull
Vestibulocochlear (VIII) functions and fiber type (slide 35)?Balance and hearing — special somatic afferent (SSA)
Vestibulocochlear (VIII) dysfunction signs (slide 37)?Ataxia, head tilt (usually toward the affected side), circling (toward the lesion), nystagmus, strabismus (ventral deviation when the head moves), nausea/vomiting, disorientation, reluctance to stand/walk, hearing loss
Head tilt and circling go which way with vestibular disease (slide 37)?Usually TOWARD the affected side
Nystagmus directions, and which suggests a central lesion (slide 37)?Horizontal, rotary, or (in severe central cases) VERTICAL
Which part of VIII causes hearing loss when damaged (slide 37)?Cochlear portion

🗝️ CN IX–XII · Origin, emergence & dissection (slides 38–39, 47–48)

Origin of IX, X, XII (slide 38)?Entirely from the MYELENCEPHALON
Why is XI 'odd' in origin (slide 38)?Small part from the myelencephalon; the majority from the spinal cord
Exit of IX, X, XI (slide 39)?Leave the cranial cavity via the JUGULAR FORAMEN (internal opening) → emerge via the TYMPANOOCCIPITAL FISSURE (external opening)
Exit of XII (slide 39)?Its own canal — the HYPOGLOSSAL CANAL
Lateral dissection near the tympanooccipital fissure — boxed structures (slide 47)?Hypoglossal nerve (XII), vagus nerve (X), distal ganglion of X, accessory nerve (XI), glossopharyngeal nerve (IX), cranial cervical ganglion, vagosympathetic trunk
Why is the hypoglossal cut in the lateral dissection figure (slide 47)?To see the other nerves
Best approach to the tympanooccipital fissure nerves, and what must you remove (slide 48)?MEDIAL view is easier than lateral; remove the LONGUS CAPITIS muscle
Structures labeled in the medial dissection (slide 48)?Cranial cervical ganglion, distal ganglion of the vagus, glossopharyngeal nerve, accessory nerve, internal carotid artery, hypoglossal nerve + lingual artery, external carotid artery, vagosympathetic nerve trunk + common carotid artery

👅 CN IX · Glossopharyngeal nerve (slide 40)

Glossopharyngeal (IX): pathway (slide 40)?Myelencephalon → JUGULAR FORAMEN → TYMPANOOCCIPITAL FISSURE → short course ventrally to the pharynx, MEDIAL to the external carotid artery
Glossopharyngeal (IX) motor (slide 40)?Pharyngeal muscles — special visceral efferent (SVE)
Glossopharyngeal (IX) tongue sensation (slide 40)?CAUDAL 1/3 of the tongue: taste (special visceral afferent, SVA) and non-taste (general somatic afferent, GSA)
Glossopharyngeal (IX) visceral sensory (slide 40)?Pharynx and CAROTID SINUS — general visceral afferent (GVA)
Glossopharyngeal (IX) parasympathetic (slide 40)?PAROTID and ZYGOMATIC salivary glands — general visceral efferent (GVE)
IX branches labeled on the lateral dissection (slide 47)?Pharyngeal branch, lingual branch, branch to the carotid sinus
L28 cross-check: pharyngeal plexus = vagus (X) + glossopharyngeal (IX). Consistent with L35?Yes — slide 40: IX motor to pharyngeal muscles; slide 43: X motor to pharynx and larynx

🌀 CN X · Vagus nerve (slides 41–43)

Vagus (X): meaning and overall role (slide 41)?'The wanderer' — innervates parts of the pharynx and larynx; the major visceral motor and sensory nerve for most body organs
Vagus (X): origin and exit (slide 42)?Arises in line with IX (myelencephalon) → JUGULAR FORAMEN → TYMPANOOCCIPITAL FISSURE
Vagus (X) 2 sensory ganglia and locations (slide 42)?PROXIMAL ganglion — inside the jugular foramen, can't be seen · DISTAL ganglion — caudal to the cranial cervical ganglion
Vagus (X) general visceral efferent (GVE) (slide 43)?Visceral motor to the heart + smooth muscle and glands of the digestive and respiratory systems
Vagus (X) general visceral afferent (GVA) (slide 43)?Sensory from the digestive and respiratory systems
Vagus (X) special visceral efferent (SVE) and why 'special' (slide 43)?Motor to skeletal muscles of the pharynx and larynx — 'special' because they develop in the branchial arches
Vagus (X) general somatic afferent (GSA) (slide 43)?External auditory canal (via the AURICULAR branch) + laryngopharynx
Vagus (X) special visceral afferent (SVA) (slide 43)?Taste from the pharynx

💪 CN XI · Accessory nerve (slides 44–45)

Accessory (XI): 2 origins (slide 44)?Myelencephalon (cranial roots) + cervical spinal cord (spinal roots) — hence 'spinal accessory'
Accessory (XI): ENTERS the cranial cavity via… and EXITS via… (slide 44)?Enters via the FORAMEN MAGNUM; exits via the JUGULAR FORAMEN → TYMPANOOCCIPITAL FISSURE
Accessory (XI) muscles (slide 44)?Sternocephalicus, brachiocephalicus, omotransversarius, and SOLE innervation to the TRAPEZIUS — special visceral efferent (SVE)
Accessory (XI) branches (slide 45)?INTERNAL branch → joins the vagus · EXTERNAL branch → the one seen in dissection

👅 CN XII · Hypoglossal nerve (slide 46)

Hypoglossal (XII): exit (slide 46)?HYPOGLOSSAL CANAL, just caudomedial to the tympanooccipital fissure
Hypoglossal (XII) course relative to vessels (slide 46)?Passes LATERAL to the external carotid artery; runs with the LINGUAL artery until the caudal edge of the hyoglossus muscle
Hypoglossal (XII) function and fiber type (slide 46)?Motor (general somatic efferent, GSE) to the intrinsic and extrinsic tongue muscles + the geniohyoideus
IX vs XII relative to the external carotid artery (slides 40, 46)?IX = MEDIAL; XII = LATERAL

🏥 Central vs peripheral deficits (slide 49)

Is a CENTRAL lesion (brain or brain stem) likely to affect only one cranial nerve (slide 49)?No — the nerves are close together and interconnected, so central lesions usually affect several
A deficit limited to one nerve, especially unilateral, suggests (slide 49)?A PERIPHERAL lesion

👁️ Eye, orbit & pupillary light reflex (slides 50–56)

Eye muscle innervation formula (slide 51)?(LR6 DO4)3 — Lateral rectus + Retractor bulbi = CN VI; Dorsal Oblique = CN IV; all others = CN III (plus levator palpebrae superioris)
Which eye muscles does CN III supply (slides 17, 51)?Dorsal, ventral, medial rectus; ventral oblique; levator palpebrae superioris
Muscles of the pupil and ciliary body: tissue type and nerve (slide 52)?SMOOTH muscle — oculomotor nerve (mainly)
Extraocular and eyelid muscles: tissue type and nerve (slide 52)?SKELETAL muscle — various nerves
Where do parasympathetic vs sympathetic eye fibers synapse (slide 52)?Parasympathetic → CILIARY ganglion; sympathetic → CRANIAL CERVICAL ganglion, then join various nerves (including oculomotor)
Example of a skeletal muscle reflex vs a smooth muscle reflex of the eye (slide 52)?Skeletal = blink reflex; smooth = pupillary light reflex
Visual + pupillary pathway, 11 numbered steps (slide 53)?1 retina → 2 prechiasmatic optic tract (optic nerve) → 3 optic chiasm → 4 optic tract → 5 lateral geniculate nucleus → 6 optic radiation (internal capsule) → [visual cortex]; reflex: 7 rostral colliculus + pretectal nuclei → 8 oculomotor nucleus → 9 ciliary ganglion; sympathetic: 10 lateral visceral efferent column (thoracic spinal cord) → 11 cranial cervical ganglion (near tympanic bulla)
Which numbered stations are vision vs pupillary light reflex (slide 53)?Vision: 1–6 → visual cortex (via lateral geniculate nucleus, metathalamus). Pupillary light reflex: 1–4 → 7 rostral colliculus/pretectal nuclei (mesencephalon) → 8 oculomotor nucleus → 9 ciliary ganglion
Is vision the same as the pupillary light reflex (slide 53)?No — separate. The retina must work for the reflex, but the animal can still be blind
Parasympathetic pupil pathway (slide 54, primate)?Pretectal olivary nucleus → bilateral to Edinger–Westphal nucleus → preganglionic fibers in CN III → ciliary ganglion → short ciliary nerves → constrictor (sphincter) pupillae
Sympathetic pupil pathway (slide 54, primate)?Preganglionic neurons at C8–T1 → sympathetic trunk → cranial (superior) cervical ganglion → postganglionic through neck/carotid plexus → orbit → through ciliary ganglion + short ciliary nerves OR long ciliary nerves → dilator pupillae
Which pupil muscle does each division supply (slides 18, 54)?Parasympathetic (III) → constrictor pupillae (constricts); sympathetic → dilator pupillae (dilates)
Pupillary light reflex basics (slide 55)?Pupils should be symmetrical; signal travels retina → CNS via the PRECHIASMATIC OPTIC TRACT; light in one eye → BOTH pupils constrict
Direct vs consensual response (slide 55)?Direct = the eye receiving light; consensual = the opposite eye
Why is there a consensual response (slide 55)?SOME prechiasmatic optic tract fibers (from the NASAL retina) cross at the optic chiasm; amount varies by species
Horner's syndrome = (slide 56)?Interruption of the sympathetic innervation (to the eye)
Horner's syndrome: 4 signs (slide 56)?MIOSIS (dilator lost) · PTOSIS (upper eyelid tone lost) · ENOPHTHALMOS (periorbital muscles relax) · PROTRUSION of the third eyelid (passive, eye sinks)
Where can a Horner's lesion be, per slide 56 (numbers from slide 53 figure)?Slide text: 7 and 8 (mesencephalon, upper motor neurons), 10 (thoracolumbar spinal cord), 11 (spinal nerves of thoracolumbar region or sympathetic trunk) — see ⚠️ FLAG on numbering

👅 Tongue innervation (slides 57–60)

Chorda tympani pathway (slides 58–59)?Leaves the FACIAL nerve → passes through the ear (petrous temporal bone, near the tympanic cavity) → joins the LINGUAL nerve (V3, mandibular)
Chorda tympani functions (slide 58)?Taste from the rostral (anterior) 2/3 of the tongue + parasympathetic efferents to the mandibular (submandibular) and sublingual glands
Tongue sensory map (slide 60)?Lingual nerve (V) = general sensory, rostral 2/3 · chorda tympani (VII) = taste, rostral 2/3 · glossopharyngeal (IX) = taste + general sensory, caudal 1/3 · vagus (X) = taste/general sensory (? — literature discrepancy) at the root
Motor to the tongue (slide 60)?Hypoglossal (XII) to all tongue skeletal muscles EXCEPT the PALATOGLOSSUS
Which 4 cranial nerves give tongue sensation (slide 60 title)?V, VII, IX, X (XII for motor)
Chorda tympani figure color code (slide 58)?Red = sympathetic, blue = parasympathetic, green = special sensory taste

Foramina visible inside the cranial cavity (slide 62)?Cribriform plate, optic canal (in chiasmatic sulcus), orbital fissure, round foramen, oval foramen, internal acoustic meatus, jugular foramen
Stylomastoid foramen location and nerve (slide 64)?CAUDODORSAL to the tympanic bulla — facial nerve (VII)
Budras schematic: A, B, C/C', D (slide 65)?A cribriform plate · B optic canal · C maxillary foramen · C' infraorbital foramen · D ethmoidal foramen
Budras schematic: E, F, G, G' (slide 65)?E orbital fissure · F round foramen / rostral alar foramen · G oval foramen · G' jugular foramen / tympanooccipital fissure
