🧭 🧬 LC25 steroid receptor pathways

4 pathways · tap one arrow at a time · ★ must know · 💭 understanding only

★ Common signaling mechanism (steroid hormone receptors)Start: Hormone made in a gland (e.g., aldosterone in the adrenal cortex)
⬇️ 1 · Where does it start?
★ Ligand is synthesized, secreted and enters circulation
⬇️ 2 · What happens to it in the blood?
★ Binds CARRIER proteins at equilibrium: most bound, some free → stable free concentration
⬇️ Why does it need a carrier?
★ Ligand is HYDROPHOBIC (lipid soluble) → can't dissolve in plasma alone
⬇️ What does the carrier buy you?
★ A vascular RESERVOIR + less renal excretion → stays in blood up to hours
⬇️ 3 · How does free ligand get into the cell?
★ DIFFUSION across vessel cell layers, interstitial fluid and the cell membrane (lipid soluble, no transporter)
⬇️ Where is the receptor waiting?
★ CYTOSOL (glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid receptors) OR NUCLEUS, already on DNA (thyroid, vitamin D3, retinoid receptors)
⬇️ 4 · What happens when ligand binds?
★ Receptor is activated and the INHIBITORY protein is released
⬇️ 5 · What does the receptor do next?
★ DIMERIZES (pairs up) and enters the nucleus through the nuclear pore
⬇️ 6 · Where does it bind DNA, and with what?
★ A SPECIFIC DNA sequence, using its DNA binding domain
⬇️ What 3 domains does every receptor have?
★ Hormone (ligand) binding · DNA binding · transcription activating
⬇️ 7 · What else must be present to start transcription?
★ CO-FACTORS / co-activators (e.g., GRIP1, SRC1–3, TRAP, SMRT) → transcription begins
⬇️ 8 · What leaves the nucleus?
★ mRNA, through the nuclear pore
⬇️ 9 · What happens in the cytoplasm?
★ Protein synthesis (translation) + modifications + trafficking
⬇️ 10 · End result?
★ CHANGE IN FUNCTION (aldosterone example: more apical Na⁺ channels, apical K⁺ channels, basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase)
⬇️ How fast, and why?
★ SLOW: minutes → hours → days, because it needs transcription AND translation (plus carrier reservoir, long diffusion)
⬇️ How does the signal END?
★ Hormone must be EXCRETED: conjugated in the liver → excreted by the kidney

★ Specificity: why only SOME cells respondStart: Hormone is in the blood and reaches EVERY cell
⬇️ Requirement 1?
★ The cell must CONTAIN the receptor (no receptor = no response)
⬇️ Requirement 2?
★ Some cells need an ANCILLARY ENZYME to make or block the active ligand
⬇️ Example: blocking a ligand?
★ 11-beta hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase converts cortisol → cortisone (inactive) → leaves the mineralocorticoid receptor for aldosterone
⬇️ Example: making a stronger ligand?
★ 5-alpha reductase converts testosterone → dihydrotestosterone (DHT) · finasteride blocks it
⬇️ Another testosterone enzyme from lecture?
💭 Aromatase converts testosterone → estradiol (covered verbally 10/23, confirm details in transcript)
⬇️ Requirement 3?
★ A specific set of GENE REGULATORY proteins (co-factors) must be present; some are cell-specific
⬇️ Bottom line?
★ Same hormone everywhere, but only cells with receptor + right enzymes + right co-factors respond

★ Aldosterone pathway (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system)Start: Decrease in renal perfusion (blood pressure falls)
⬇️ Who senses it and releases what?
★ Kidney juxtaglomerular apparatus releases RENIN (S17; S18 says macula densa, which is part of it)
⬇️ Where does angiotensinogen come from?
★ The LIVER (secretes it into blood)
⬇️ Renin's reaction? Blocker?
★ Angiotensinogen → angiotensin I · aliskiren blocks renin
⬇️ Next reaction? Enzyme, location, blocker?
★ Angiotensin I → angiotensin II by angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) on the surface of PULMONARY and renal endothelium · captopril blocks
⬇️ Angiotensin II receptor? Blocker?
💭 AT1 receptor (angiotensin II type 1) · losartan (valsartan = biased)
⬇️ Angiotensin II: name all 5 effects
★ ① ↑ sympathetic activity ② tubular NaCl reabsorption + K⁺ excretion + water retention ③ adrenal cortex → aldosterone secretion ④ arteriolar vasoconstriction → BP ↑ ⑤ posterior pituitary → ADH (vasopressin) secretion
⬇️ What does ADH do?
★ Collecting duct WATER absorption
⬇️ Aldosterone: source, carrier, receptor, blocker?
★ Adrenal cortex · albumin, transcortin (<25%) · cytosolic MINERALOCORTICOID receptor · spironolactone
⬇️ Which proteins does aldosterone make?
★ Apical Na⁺ channel · apical K⁺ channel · basolateral Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase
⬇️ Net effect in the kidney tubule?
★ Na⁺, Cl⁻ and water RETAINED · K⁺ EXCRETED (amiloride blocks the Na⁺ channel)
⬇️ Whole-body result?
★ Water and salt retention → effective circulating volume ↑ → blood pressure ↑
⬇️ How does it turn itself off?
★ Perfusion of the juxtaglomerular apparatus ↑ → INHIBITS renin release (negative feedback)

Thyroid axis: cold/stress → metabolism ↑Start: Cold and/or stress
⬇️ Hypothalamus releases?
★ TRH (thyrotropin releasing hormone) → portal circulation to the anterior pituitary
⬇️ Pituitary releases?
★ TSH (thyroid stimulating hormone = thyrotropin) from thyrotrophs
⬇️ Thyroid releases?
★ Mostly T4 (thyroxine, the PROHORMONE), some T3
⬇️ Carriers in blood?
★ Transthyretin + thyroxine-binding globulin
⬇️ How does T4 become active? Where?
★ De-iodinases remove one iodine → T3 (ACTIVE) · mainly kidney and liver
⬇️ Receptor and location?
★ Thyroid hormone receptor · NUCLEAR (already on DNA)
⬇️ Effects?
★ General ↑ metabolism: ↑ O₂ consumption and ventilation · thermogenesis · fat and protein mobilization
⬇️ Negative feedback?
★ T4 inhibits TSH release from the pituitary (slide wording)
