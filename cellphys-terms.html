🧬 LC14 · Amino acids & protein structure

Amino acid partsAmine group + carboxylic acid group + a side chain (R group) that sets its class.
Positively charged amino acidsLysine, arginine, histidine.
Negatively charged amino acidsAspartate and glutamate.
Polar uncharged amino acidsSerine, threonine, tyrosine (alcohol groups) + asparagine and glutamine.
Phosphorylatable amino acidsSerine, threonine, tyrosine: the OH (alcohol) groups take the phosphate.
Nonpolar (hydrophobic) amino acidsEverything else (e.g., phenylalanine, leucine, valine); they hide in the protein core.
Primary structureThe amino acid sequence; direct translation of mRNA. Written N-terminus left → C-terminus right.
Secondary structureLocal folds (alpha helix, beta sheet) held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
Tertiary structureWhole chain collapses into one 3D shape, driven mainly by hydrophobic interactions between side chains.
Disulfide bondCovalent bond between two cysteines that locks (stabilizes) tertiary structure.
Quaternary structureSeveral polypeptide chains forming one protein (hemoglobin = 2 alpha + 2 beta). Only some proteins.
Posttranslational modificationChanging a side chain after translation (e.g., phosphorylation) to change its properties.
AntibodyDefense protein that recognizes the unique 3D surface of one specific protein (its antigen).
AntigenThe molecule (usually a protein) an antibody recognizes.
ImmunohistochemistryAn antibody probes a tissue section for a specific protein.
Direct fluorescent antibody testType of immunohistochemistry: fluorescent anti-rabies antibody on brain tissue → glows if rabies protein is present.
Agglutination assayAn antigen probes serum for antibodies; antibodies cross-link it → clumps (precipitate).
Tube agglutination (Brucella suis)Brucella antigen + pig serum → clumping if anti-Brucella antibodies. Titer > 1/160 = infection.
TiterHighest serum dilution that still reacts; higher titer = more antibody.
ELISAEnzyme-linked immunosorbent assay: an enzyme multiplies the signal → very sensitive, but more false positives.
Coggins testAgar immunodiffusion (agglutination-type) test for equine infectious anemia; used to confirm a positive ELISA.
Western blotUsed when ELISA and Coggins disagree on equine infectious anemia.

⚙️ LC15 · Protein regulation & enzymes

4 ways to regulate a proteinSorting (where), conformation (shape), degradation (want it?), zymogen activation (when).
Protein sortingSignal peptide or targeting sequence sends a protein to a location; no tag = stays in cytoplasm.
Signal peptideUsually hydrophobic N-terminal sequence: "not a cytoplasm protein" → ER / secretory path.
Conformational changeLigand binding creates new forces that spread through the secondary structure → big shape/activity change.
UbiquitinCommon 76-amino-acid tag; attached by ubiquitin-protein ligase → sends protein to the proteasome.
ProteasomeLarge multiprotein complex that breaks proteins down.
N-end ruleThe N-terminal sequence partly decides whether a protein gets ubiquitinated.
Zymogen (proenzyme)Inactive form of an enzyme (often a protease) that must be cleaved to turn on.
Glucocorticoid receptor at restSits in the cytoplasm bound to a heat shock protein complex (HSP70/HSP90), inactive.
Glucocorticoid receptor activationCortisol binds → shape change releases HSP → dimer → nucleus → immunosuppressive gene transcription.
Glucocorticoid receptor off switchUbiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome.
Dexamethasone vs RU486Dexamethasone = glucocorticoid receptor agonist. RU486 (mifepristone) = antagonist.
ΔG (Gibbs free energy change)Energy of products − reactants. Predicts DIRECTION, not speed.
Negative ΔGReleases energy, runs forward (spontaneous, exergonic). Very negative = essentially irreversible.
EquilibriumΔG = 0: no net energy available, no net direction.
Activation energy (Ea)Energy to reach the high-energy intermediate. Predicts SPEED: higher Ea = slower.
CatalystLowers Ea, speeds the reaction, is not used up, and does NOT change ΔG.
EnzymeBiological (usually protein) catalyst; speeds up AND regulates reactions. Ribosome = RNA enzyme.
KmSubstrate concentration at ½ maximum velocity. Low Km = high affinity.
Near Km vs far above KmNear Km: rate is very sensitive to substrate. Far above Km: saturated, rate barely changes.
Rate-limiting stepSlowest step in a pathway; sets how fast the whole pathway runs.
Committed stepReaction that commits a molecule to one pathway.
Substrate-level regulationRate set only by how much reactant vs product is around.
Allosteric regulationMolecule binds AWAY from the active site and activates or inhibits the enzyme.
Feedback inhibitionA later product inhibits an earlier enzyme → prevents overproducing the end product.
Feed-forward activationAn earlier reactant activates a later enzyme → prevents build-up of starting material.
Equine polysaccharide storage myopathy type 1Dominant glycogen synthase mutation → active WITHOUT glucose-6-phosphate → glycogen builds in muscle (↑AST, ↑CK).
EPSM1 managementNo cure: high fat, low carbohydrate diet + daily turnout/exercise; don't breed (dominant).

🍬 LC16 · Glucose transport, glycolysis, anaerobic metabolism

GLUTGlucose transporter family; passive (facilitated diffusion) DOWN the glucose gradient.
GLUT4The insulin-dependent GLUT (muscle, adipose). The one GLUT you must know.
GLUT1 / GLUT3 tissuesBrain and red blood cells: tissues with an absolute glucose requirement.
SGLTSodium-glucose cotransporter: secondary active, uses the Na⁺ gradient to move glucose UP its gradient (gut, kidney).
Glucose trappingHexokinase/glucokinase adds a phosphate → glucose-6-phosphate is negative and can't leave; keeps free glucose low inside.
HexokinaseLow Km (<1 mM, high affinity), most tissues, constitutive, inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate.
GlucokinaseHigh Km (~15 mM), liver + pancreatic β-cells, inducible, NOT inhibited; glucose sensor.
4 → 7.5 mM glucoseHexokinase activity +1.7%; glucokinase activity +58%.
Glycolysis first half (steps 1–5)"Add a phosphate, change around, add a phosphate, break it down."
Glycolysis second half (steps 6–10)"NADH, ATP, switch it, dry it, ATP."
Phosphofructokinase-1Main regulatory, irreversible, rate-limiting step of glycolysis (step 3).
Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatePFK-1 product; feed-forward activates pyruvate kinase.
Pyruvate kinaseLast step (PEP → pyruvate); irreversible, regulated, makes ATP.
Irreversible glycolysis stepsHexokinase/glucokinase, phosphofructokinase-1, pyruvate kinase.
Glycolysis net yield+2 ATP and +2 NADH per glucose (~7 ATP with oxygen).
Lactate dehydrogenaseReduces pyruvate → lactate to regenerate NAD⁺ (or reverses to clear lactate).
Why make lactate?To refill NAD⁺ so glycolysis step 6 keeps running when oxygen is low.
Lactic acid drawbacksOnly 2 ATP, loses TCA energy, builds in blood → metabolic acidosis.
Cori cycleMuscle lactate → liver → glucose → back to muscle. Net cost 4 ATP (+2 muscle, −6 liver).
Lactate in the heartOxidative muscle takes up lactate → pyruvate → TCA; up to 70% of heart energy in intense exercise.
Yeast fermentationPyruvate decarboxylase + alcohol dehydrogenase → ethanol, regenerating NAD⁺.
Volatile fatty acidsAcetate, propionate (+ butyrate) from rumen bacteria = main ruminant energy source.
Hindgut fermentersHorses, rabbits.
Pyruvate kinase deficiency (Westie)Red cells have no mitochondria → glycolysis only → no ATP → hemolytic anemia. No cure; transfusions.
Grain overload (ruminant)Fast fermentation acidifies rumen → Lactobacillus takes over → lactic acid → ↓ rumen contractions, acidosis.

🔥 LC17 · TCA cycle, redox, electron transport

TCA cycleTricarboxylic acid cycle = citric acid cycle = Krebs cycle; inside the mitochondria.
TCA orderCitrate → isocitrate → α-ketoglutarate → succinyl-CoA → succinate → fumarate → malate → oxaloacetate.
Acetyl-CoAPyruvate minus a CO₂; enters the cycle with oxaloacetate to make citrate. "Remember forever."
OxaloacetateNeeded for step 1; made by step 8. Also used by gluconeogenesis.
TCA yield per glucose+2 GTP, +8 NADH, +2 FADH₂ (~25 ATP equivalents).
Why the TCA cycle needs O₂No step uses O₂, but O₂ (via the ETS) regenerates NAD⁺ and FAD.
Step 8 (malate → oxaloacetate)Large positive ΔG + makes NADH → without O₂ it runs backward → oxaloacetate runs out.
IschemiaToo little blood flow → no O₂ → succinate builds up from BOTH directions.
Reperfusion injuryO₂ returns → stored succinate → fumarate fast → FADH₂ surge → ETS → oxidative damage.
OxidationLosing electrons (more positive charge, gaining a bond to oxygen, losing H⁺ + e⁻).
Reduction"Reduce the positive charge": gaining electrons (gaining H⁺ + e⁻, losing a bond to oxygen).
Reducer vs oxidizerReducer gives electrons and is oxidized; oxidizer takes electrons and is reduced.
Coupled redoxEvery oxidation is paired with a reduction; NADH → NAD⁺ is coupled to O₂ → water.
Electron transport system locationInner mitochondrial membrane (impervious to H⁺).
Complex ITakes NADH electrons → NAD⁺; pumps protons.
Complex IISuccinate dehydrogenase (succinate → fumarate, FADH₂); does NOT pump protons.
Coenzyme QCarries electrons from complexes I and II to complex III.
Cytochrome cCarries electrons from complex III to complex IV.
Complex IVHands electrons to O₂ → water; pumps protons.
Intermembrane spaceGap between mitochondrial membranes where pumped protons pile up.
Proton motive forceStored energy of the proton gradient; drives ATP synthase.
ATP synthaseProtons flow through and TURN a channel → shape change → ADP + phosphate → ATP.
ATP synthase numbers10 H⁺ per full turn = 3 ATP (3.33 H⁺/ATP).
NADH vs FADH₂ yieldNADH ~10 H⁺ → ~2.5 ATP. FADH₂ ~6 H⁺ → ~1.5 ATP.
Malate/aspartate shuttleCarries electrons from cytoplasm NADH into the mitochondria.
Superoxide (O₂•⁻)Oxygen with one extra electron; very damaging. Made in the reperfusion surge.
Cat saddle thrombus caseClot buster restores flow → reperfusion injury → death hours later.

🍯 LC18 · Glycogen & gluconeogenesis

GlycogenHighly branched glucose polymer (α-1,4 chains, α-1,6 branches), built on glycogenin.
Glycogen storesLiver 5–8% by weight; muscle 1–2%, but muscle holds MORE in total (more mass).
OsmolyteDissolved particle that pulls water; free glucose is one → stored glucose would burst cells.
Why glycogen, not fat?Fat can't become glucose; glycogen mobilizes faster and can be used anaerobically.
Glycogen vs fat energy4 kcal/g vs 9 kcal/g.
Glycogen synthaseRegulatory enzyme of glycogen synthesis (adds UDP-glucose).
Glycogen branching enzymeMoves 7 residues to make α-1,6 branches.
Glycogen phosphorylaseRegulatory enzyme of glycogen breakdown; cuts off glucose-1-phosphate.
Glycogen debranching enzymeClears branch points so phosphorylase can keep cutting.
Glucose-6-phosphataseLiver only: glucose-6-phosphate → free glucose to export (muscle lacks it).
Insulin on glycogenProtein phosphatase 1 dephosphorylates → synthase ON, phosphorylase OFF → store.
Glucagon on glycogencAMP → protein kinase A → phosphorylase kinase → phosphorylase ON; synthase phosphorylated OFF → break down.
GluconeogenesisMaking new glucose from non-carbohydrates; mainly liver, kidney under stress.
Gluconeogenic substratesLactate, alanine (most amino acids), glycerol, propionate. NOT fatty acids.
Glycerol entry pointEnters at dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), not pyruvate; net energy positive.
PropionateRumen VFA → propionyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA → oxaloacetate → glucose; cow's main glucose source.
Pyruvate carboxylaseBypass 1a: pyruvate → oxaloacetate.
PEP carboxykinasePhosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, bypass 1b: oxaloacetate → phosphoenolpyruvate.
Fructose bisphosphatase-1Regulatory enzyme of gluconeogenesis; bypasses PFK-1.
PFK-2/FBPase-2One enzyme, two jobs: makes or destroys fructose-2,6-bisphosphate.
Fructose-2,6-bisphosphateAllosteric: ACTIVATES PFK-1 (glycolysis) and INHIBITS FBPase-1 (gluconeogenesis).
Glucagon on PFK-2/FBPase-2PKA phosphorylates → phosphatase half ON → ↓F2,6BP → gluconeogenesis.
Insulin on PFK-2/FBPase-2PP1 dephosphorylates → kinase half ON → ↑F2,6BP → glycolysis.
Glycogen storage disease IIIaCurly-coated retriever, recessive debranching enzyme defect → hepatomegaly + hypoglycemia.
Type 1 ketosis (dairy cow)Post-calving negative energy balance; gluconeogenic precursors run out → ketones. Treat with simple carbs.

🥩 LC19 · Amino acid catabolism & nitrogen waste

Glucogenic amino acidCan be converted to glucose (most of them; e.g., alanine → pyruvate).
Ketogenic-only amino acidsLeucine and lysine.
Both glucogenic and ketogenicPhenylalanine, tyrosine, threonine, isoleucine, tryptophan.
Why acetyl-CoA can't make glucoseIts 2 carbons leave as CO₂ in the TCA cycle; no net carbon for oxaloacetate.
TransaminationAmine moves from an amino acid onto α-ketoglutarate → glutamate + an α-keto acid.
DeaminationGlutamate releases NH₄⁺ (regenerating α-ketoglutarate).
α-keto acidAmino acid with the amine swapped for oxygen = a carbohydrate skeleton.
Branched-chain amino acidsLeucine, isoleucine, valine; up to ⅓ of muscle protein; broken down in muscle, not liver.
Aromatic amino acidsPhenylalanine, tyrosine; preferentially broken down by the liver.
BCAA:AAA ratioLiver-function marker: normal ~3:1; liver disease can drop to ~1.5:1.
Ammonia toxicityBase (↑pH); NH₄⁺ is K⁺-sized → K⁺ dysregulation in brain; ↑glutamine.
Urea cycleLiver: 2 NH₄⁺ + HCO₃⁻ + 3 ATP → urea (safer, not basic). Slightly energy-positive when TCA runs.
Carbamoyl phosphate synthetaseRegulatory step of the urea cycle.
N-acetylglutamateAllosteric activator of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase; made when glutamate is high.
CreatineAlternate nitrogen waste (from glycine + urea-cycle nitrogen); stores high-energy phosphate in muscle.
CreatinineSpontaneous breakdown product of creatine/phosphocreatine; always entering blood.
Hepatic encephalopathyBrain dysfunction from liver failure: ↑ammonia + low BCAA:AAA.
Wolfhound case↑NH₄⁺, ↑serum bile acids, ↓BCAA:AAA → patent ductus venosus (portosystemic shunt).
Hepatic encephalopathy dietHigh-quality, low-protein diet enriched in branched-chain amino acids.

🧈 LC20 · Lipid metabolism & ketones

LipogenesisStoring energy as fat: fatty acid then triglyceride synthesis (liver + adipose).
Acetyl-CoA carboxylaseCommitted, rate-limiting step of fatty acid synthesis: acetyl-CoA → malonyl-CoA.
Fatty acid synthesis carrierUses NADPH (not NADH); adds 2 carbons at a time.
ACC turned ON byHigh citrate (feed-forward) + insulin dephosphorylation.
ACC turned OFF byPhosphorylation by AMP-activated kinase, glucagon (PKA), or epinephrine (PKA).
Triglyceride synthesisGlycerol-3-phosphate (from DHAP) + 3 fatty acids; liver ships it out as VLDL.
VLDLVery low density lipoprotein: carries liver triglyceride to the body.
LipolysisUsing fat for energy: hormone-sensitive lipase, then β-oxidation.
Hormone-sensitive lipaseMain lipolysis enzyme; cuts fatty acids off glycerol. Phosphorylation turns it ON.
Carnitine shuttleMoves fatty acyl-CoA into the mitochondria (it can't cross alone).
β-oxidation stepsAcyl-CoA dehydrogenase (FADH₂) → enoyl-CoA hydratase → 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase (NADH) → thiolase.
ThiolaseCuts off acetyl-CoA; chain shortens by 2 carbons per round.
Palmitate yield16-carbon fat → ~106 ATP.
Epinephrine / glucagon on fatPKA phosphorylates → HSL ON, ACC OFF → burn fat.
Insulin on fatProtein phosphatase dephosphorylates → HSL OFF, ACC ON → store fat.
Ketogenesis triggerOxaloacetate (used by gluconeogenesis) and free CoA run out.
Ketogenesis purposeRegenerates free CoA; ketones fuel heart and brain. Liver and kidney.
Ketone bodiesAcetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone.
Liver can't use ketonesLacks 3-oxoacid CoA transferase.
Ketone levelsNormal ~0.1 mM · ketosis > 0.5 mM (normal) · ketoacidosis > 1.5 mM (pH < 7.35).
Diabetic ketoacidosisGlucose can't enter cells (no insulin) → cells "starve" → burn fat → ketones.

📡 LC21 · Cell signaling

LigandAny molecule that binds a specific site on a protein.
ReceptorProtein that recognizes a signal (ligand) and responds, usually by signal transduction.
AgonistLigand that binds selectively and triggers a physiological response.
AntagonistLigand that binds selectively and blocks or changes another ligand's action.
Second messengerSignal made inside the cell after transduction (cAMP, cGMP, IP3, DAG, Ca²⁺).
Selective vs specificReceptors PREFER ligands (selective) but aren't truly specific → cross-reactions.
Gap junctionConnexin channels (connexons) linking cells; pass cAMP (liver glucagon) or current (heart).
Contact-dependent signalingSignal stays on the cell surface (leukocyte adhesion; β-neurexin–neuroligin synapse).
Leukocyte rollingPSGL-1 on leukocytes binds P-selectin → loose rolling; integrins + ICAM-1 → firm adhesion.
EndocrineSignal travels in the blood; reaches most cells; low concentration (10⁻⁸ M epinephrine).
NeuroendocrineEndocrine where the signaling cell is a neuron (vasopressin from the posterior pituitary).
ParacrineSignal diffuses to nearby cells (histamine, NO).
AutocrineSignal acts back on the same cell that released it.
Synaptic signalingReaches selected cells; high concentration needed (10⁻⁴ M norepinephrine).
Vasopressin (ADH)Released for ↑ blood osmolality or ↓ volume. Pigs and marsupials have LYSINE vasopressin.
V1 receptorVascular smooth muscle → IP3 + DAG → contraction → ↑ blood pressure.
V2 receptorKidney collecting duct → ↑cAMP → aquaporins inserted → water retention.
Oxytocin (Pitocin)Close cousin of vasopressin → can hit vasopressin receptors → fluid retention, ↑BP.
Histamine → vasodilationH1 on endothelium → nitric oxide → smooth muscle guanylyl cyclase → cGMP → relaxation.
Nitric oxideGas; diffuses into cells; targets the intracellular enzyme guanylyl cyclase.
Neuromuscular junctionACh → nicotinic ACh receptor (ionotropic) → depolarization → ↑Ca²⁺ → contraction.
Water-soluble signalsACh, epinephrine, glycine, LH, FSH → cell-surface receptors.
Lipid-soluble signalsAldosterone, thyroid hormone (steroids, vitamin D) → carrier proteins in plasma → intracellular receptors.
AldosteroneSteroid → mineralocorticoid receptor → SGK1 → Na⁺ + water retention, K⁺ secretion (new protein = slow).
Signaling molecule typesProteins, peptides, amino acids, nucleotides, fatty acid/cholesterol derivatives, small molecules, gases, ions.

🔄 LC23 · Integration of metabolism (Mon 10/19)

Integration of metabolismWhich pathways run when, in which tissue, under which hormone.
5 metabolic statesFed (just ate) · early fasting (1–6 h) · late fasting (6–48 h) · starvation (>48 h) · exercise.
Fed state · hormonesHigh INSULIN, low glucagon.
Fed state · bloodFull of glucose, triglycerides and amino acids.
Fed state · liverMakes glycogen, fatty acids and triglycerides.
Fed state · adiposeMakes triglycerides.
Fed state · muscleMakes glycogen and proteins.
Fed state · brainBurns glucose.
Early fasting · hormonesInsulin falling, glucagon rising.
Early fasting · liverBreaks down glycogen to put glucose into the blood.
Early fasting · adiposeBreaks down triglycerides → fatty acids + glycerol into blood.
Early fasting · muscleBreaks down its glycogen; burns glucose and fatty acids.
Late fasting · hormonesLow insulin, high GLUCAGON.
Late fasting · liverGlycogen depleted → gluconeogenesis (glucogenic amino acids, glycerol) + ketogenesis (ketogenic amino acids). Same as a ketogenic diet.
Late fasting · muscleBreaks down protein → amino acids into the blood.
Late fasting · brainBurns glucose AND ketone bodies.
Starvation · hormonesVery low insulin, high (but moderating) glucagon.
Starvation · bloodFalling glucose, high fatty acids and glycerol.
Starvation · liverGluconeogenesis from glycerol; β-oxidation → ketone bodies.
Starvation · kidneyJoins in: gluconeogenesis from glycerol.
Starvation · muscleWasting; burns fatty acids.
Starvation · brainBurns ketone bodies.
Starvation time courseExogenous glucose → liver glycogen (first day) → gluconeogenesis → ketone bodies climb over days.
Exercise · hormoneEPINEPHRINE.
Exercise · bloodOxygen delivery falls behind muscle use.
Exercise · muscleBurns glucose + fatty acids; more fuel than oxygen → makes lactate.
Exercise · liverGluconeogenesis from lactate (Cori cycle), during and after exercise.
Exercise · adiposeBreaks down triglycerides → fatty acids + glycerol into blood.
Glycolysis controlPFK-1, activated by fructose-2,6-bisphosphate; PFK-2/FBPase-2 dephosphorylated; insulin → protein phosphatases.
Gluconeogenesis controlFBPase-1, freed from fructose-2,6-bisphosphate inhibition; PFK-2/FBPase-2 phosphorylated; glucagon/epinephrine → PKA.
Glycogenesis controlGlycogen synthase, ON when dephosphorylated; insulin → protein phosphatases.
Glycogenolysis controlGlycogen phosphorylase, ON when phosphorylated; glucagon/epinephrine → PKA.
Lipogenesis controlAcetyl-CoA carboxylase, ON when dephosphorylated; insulin → protein phosphatases.
Lipolysis controlHormone-sensitive lipase, ON when phosphorylated; glucagon/epinephrine → PKA.
Growth hormone → IGF-1Growth hormone raises insulin-like growth factor 1 → more protein synthesis.
Testosterone (anabolic steroid)Synergizes with growth hormone: ↑IGF-1, ↑protein synthesis.
Insulin and proteinAnabolic by INHIBITING protein breakdown; insulin insensitivity blocks much of the growth hormone effect.
Thyroid hormone (T3)Triiodothyronine: ↑protein synthesis + ↑growth hormone, but ↑breakdown at supraphysiological levels.
Glucocorticoids (cortisol)Catabolic: increase protein breakdown.

🧪 LC08 · Acids, bases & ion trapping (Wed 10/21)

pHNegative log of the H⁺ concentration: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].
pH example1 mM (10⁻³ M) HCl → pH 3.
Strong acidDissociates FULLY in water (HCl). H₂SO₄ is strong for its first proton.
Weak acidOnly partly dissociates; e.g., HSO₄⁻ (pKa 1.9).
Acid (Brønsted–Lowry)Any molecule that DONATES a proton (H⁺).
Base (Brønsted–Lowry)Any molecule that ACCEPTS a proton (H⁺).
Conjugate pairAcid form is always the protonated one; one of the pair carries a net charge.
KaAcid dissociation constant = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA].
pKa−log Ka; the pH where [HA] = [A⁻] (50:50).
pH below pKaProtonated form (HA) dominates.
pH above pKaDeprotonated form (A⁻) dominates.
Henderson–HasselbalchpH − pKa = log([A⁻]/[HA]) → 10^(pH − pKa) = [A⁻]/[HA].
Each pH unitA 10-fold change in the ratio (log scale).
Buffer pKa on a titration curveThe inflection point.
Water (Kw)Weak acid and weak base: [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ × 10⁻⁷ = 10⁻¹⁴.
Normal pHPlasma/extracellular fluid ~7.4; cytosol ~7.2.
Mass actionChange one component of a reaction → the others shift.
Bicarbonate bufferHCO₃⁻ / H₂CO₃, pKa 6.4.
Phosphate bufferHPO₄²⁻ / H₂PO₄⁻, pKa 7.2.
Deoxyhemoglobin bufferpKa 7–7.7, from its histidines.
Ideal bufferpKa near the target pH + high capacity (many H⁺ sites).
Why body buffers work anywayPlentiful, and their components can be adjusted physiologically.
Bicarbonate response to added H⁺H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → H₂CO₃ → H₂O + CO₂; breathing faster vents CO₂ and pulls it further right.
Carbonic anhydraseEnzyme catalyzing carbonic acid ⇄ water + CO₂.
logP (octanol:water)Lipid-solubility measure. Uncharged = positive logP, crosses membranes. Charged = negative logP, doesn't.
Ion trappingUncharged form crosses the membrane; on the side where it ionizes, it gets stuck and accumulates.
Weak acid · when ionizedAt pH ABOVE its pKa (A⁻).
Weak base · when ionizedAt pH BELOW its pKa (BH⁺).
Weak acid drugs (examples)Aspirin 3.5, warfarin 5.0, amoxicillin 2.4, phenytoin 8.4.
Weak base drugs (examples)Lidocaine 7.9, codeine 8.2, epinephrine 8.7, atropine 9.7.
Body fluid pH (examples)Stomach 1.5 · urine 5.8 · intracellular 6.8 · CSF 7.3 · blood 7.4 · pancreatic 8.0 · bile 8.5.
Aspirin in the stomachpKa 3.5, pH 2 → mostly protonated (uncharged) → absorbed.
Aspirin in extracellular fluidpH 7 → mostly ionized → trapped there.
Aspirin practice ratios (worked out)Base/acid: stomach 10^(2−3.5) ≈ 0.03; extracellular 10^(7−3.5) ≈ 3,160.
