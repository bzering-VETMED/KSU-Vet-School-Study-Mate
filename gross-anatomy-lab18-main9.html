❓ Name it (complete + specific)
Cervicothoracic ganglion
📍 Where?
Thoracic inlet, on the longus colli (fused caudal cervical + 1st thoracic ganglia)
🌿 Gives?
Ansa subclavia, vertebral n., cardiac nn., rami to C7–T2
📝 Slide note (professor)
Note:
The sympathetic trunk likely has identified in previous labs, it is found near the thoracic duct.

Following the sympathetic trunk cranially, near the level of the first rib, you should find an enlargement of this trunk that is giving off multiple branches. This enlargement is the cervicothoracic ganglion.

Dissection:
You will need to pick through the fat and fascia along the dorsum to follow the sympathetic nerve trunk. Be cautious not to inadvertently transect the thoracic duct or nearby intercostal arteries. If you are struggling to find the ganglion cranially, it may help to transect the ribs further dorsally to allow for better reflection of the costal wall. There also will likely be large amounts of fat that you will need to pick through to find the enlargement of the ganglion, once you find the ganglion, notice the many branches coming from it which will be further discussed shortly in this lab.

Say it out loud: view, recumbency, cranial/caudal, dorsal/ventral. Then check the orientation tags on the image.
❓ Name it (complete + specific)
Ansa subclavia
🧩 What?
Sympathetic loop around the SUBCLAVIAN A.: cervicothoracic ganglion ↔ middle cervical ganglion
📝 Slide note (professor)
Note:
Cranial to the cervicothoracic ganglion, the sympathetic trunk divides to form a loop, this loop is called the ansa subclavia. It is named according Ansa meaning loop and subclavia as it is looping around the subclavian arteries.

The loop comes together at the middle cervical ganglion.

There will be a sympathetic trunk, cervicothoracic ganglion, ansa subclava, middle cervical ganglion on both sides of the body.

Dissection:
Dig through the fat and fascia near the dorsal body wall to find the ansa subclavia. Sometimes it is easier to start with tracing the sympathetic trunk cranially to the cervicothoracic ganglion or tracing the vagosympathetic nerve caudally to the middle cervical ganglion to find this structure.

📝 Slide note (professor)

Once you have found ansa subclavia, this should lead you directly to the middle cervical ganglion.

In felines, the middle cervical ganglion often is very small to non-existent making it very difficult to find.

CN X: parasympathetic (+ sensory) to thoracic + abdominal viscera
📝 Slide note (professor)
Note:
Vagus nerve or cranial nerve X, contains parasympathetic preganglionic axons and it courses caudally down the neck to thoracic and abdominal organs. At the level of the middle cervical ganglion it continues caudally, also remember cardiac nerves also leave from this ganglion.

The vagus nerve is further divided into several sections which will be identified shortly.

You may have inadvertently transected the vagus nerve as you were removing the lungs, if that is the case, try to find the stumps left of vagus nerve.

Left recurrent laryngeal nerve
🔁 Loops around?
AORTIC ARCH (caudal to the ligamentum arteriosum) → back up the trachea
➡️ Becomes?
Caudal laryngeal n.
📝 Slide note (professor)
Note:
Near the level of the aortic arch, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve leaves the vagus nerve and curves medially around the ligamentum arteriosum and the arch of the aorta and becomes ventrolateral to the trachea. It then courses cranially in the neck to reach the larynx. Near the larynx in earlier labs, you may have seen this nerve within the carotid sheath, notice the size difference between this nerve and the larger vagosympathetic trunk.

The left recurrent laryngeal nerve branches off the left vagus and curves cranially further caudal than the right recurrent laryngeal. Figure 5-57 of the lab guide showcases this.

Dissection:
Find the left recurrent laryngeal nerve near the aortic arch and follow it cranially, there will be fascia and other structures in this area that will need to be removed in order to visualize this nerve. This nerve may have already be inadvertently transected, if that happens, try to find the stump that was left of it. This image was taken from VAP806 Pre-lab videos

📍 Find + point to: Vagus nerve📚 course slide · Lab 18 slides #51
🧭 Orient first
Say it out loud: view, recumbency, cranial/caudal, dorsal/ventral. Then check the orientation tags on the image.
🧩 What?
CN X: parasympathetic (+ sensory) to thoracic + abdominal viscera
📝 Slide note (professor)
Note:
Follow the vagus nerve as it courses over the heart, giving off cardiac branches. The vagus nerves are bilateral, each nerve gives off a dorsal and a ventral branch. These branches right and left dorsal branches unite and the right and left ventral branches unite caudally to form vagal trunks. These trunks are found on the dorsal and ventral surfaces of the esophagus. The dorsal trunk unites much further caudally than the ventral trunk, the dorsal trunk is often very hard to visualize as it unites deep within the thoracic cavity right before passing through the diaphragm.

Dissection:
You will need to dissect out the vagus nerves and their branches to find where they unite to form the trunks. This is a tedious process of picking through fascia to follow these nerves. These nerves may also have already been transected if when removing the lungs, it was cut too close to the heart. If that is the case, try to identify the stumps of each nerve and find the trunks. (Figure 5-57)
