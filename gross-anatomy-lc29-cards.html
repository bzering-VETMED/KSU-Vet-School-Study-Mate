🦷 LO1 · Tooth type & hard tissues

Brachydont tooth — definition and features (slide 6)?'Brachy' = short; dogs + cats; does NOT grow once erupted; crown (above gum) + neck + root (in the alveolus)
Brachydont vs hypsodont on the diagram (slides 6–7)?Brachydont = short crown, distinct crown/neck/root (dog, cat). Hypsodont = 'high' tooth: long crown, short root, NO distinct neck, continuously erupting (horse, cattle cheek teeth)
Diagram legend for tooth tissues (slide 6)?Pulp cavity (black), bone, enamel, dentine, cementum.
Three regions of a brachydont tooth (slides 6, 8)?Crown, neck, root.
Enamel — hardness, mineral %, collagen, secreting cell (slide 8)?Hardest component of the body; 96% hydroxyapatite; NO collagen; secreted by ameloblasts.
Dentin — hardness, mineral %, collagen, secreting cell (slide 9)?Harder than bone; 70% hydroxyapatite; type I collagen present; secreted by odontoblasts. Collagen gives tensile strength + flexibility and spreads chewing forces.
Cementum — hardness, mineral %, collagen, secreting cell (slide 10)?Resembles bone; 45% hydroxyapatite; type I collagen present; secreted by cementoblasts.
Rank enamel, dentin, cementum by hydroxyapatite content (slides 8–10)…Enamel 96% > dentin 70% > cementum 45%.
Which hard tooth tissue lacks collagen (slides 8–10)?Enamel (dentin and cementum both have type I collagen).
Labels on the tooth section figure (slides 8–12)?Enamel, gingiva, dentin, pulp cavity, root canal, cementum, periodontal ligaments, dental alveolus; crown, neck, root.
Why float a horse's teeth?Hypsodont teeth keep erupting → grind down sharp enamel points with a float (speculum holds the mouth open)

🩸 LO1 · Pulp cavity

Two parts of the pulp cavity and where each sits (slide 11)?Pulp chamber = in the crown + neck; root canal = in the root.
Contents of the pulp cavity (slide 11)?Sensory nerves, arteries, veins, and lymphatic capillaries.
Opening at the root tip where pulp vessels/nerves enter (slide 11)?Apical foramen.
Figure 03-13 (simple tooth, longitudinal section) numbered labels (slides 12, 19)?1 enamel; 2 dentin; 3 cement(um); 4 pulp; 5 apical foramen; 6 periodontal ligament; 7 socket (alveolus); 8 gum.
Clinical application on slide 12 shows…Photos of a dog's canine teeth being examined beside the tooth-section figure — pulp/apical foramen context (clinical point in transcript; slides show images only).
Root canal procedure?Clean out an infected/inflamed/damaged pulp cavity (vessels + nerves) and fill it

🌸 LO1 · Periodontium & gingiva

Periodontium — definition (slide 13)?The supporting tissues of the teeth.
Four components of the periodontium (slide 13)?1 Gums (gingivae), 2 cementum on the root, 3 periodontal ligament, 4 alveolar bone.
Gums (gingivae) — what they do and are made of (slide 14)?Surround the teeth; dense fibrous tissue covered by mucous membrane.
Gingival margin (slide 15)?The edge of the gingiva NOT attached to the crown (free gingival margin on the photo).
Gingival sulcus (slide 15)?Space between the gingival margin and the tooth.
Normal gingival sulcus depth — dog vs cat (slide 15)?Dog 1–3 mm; cat 0.5–1 mm.
Sulcus depth at which a dog's tooth is 'in danger' (slide 16)?> 4 mm (normal 1–3 mm); measured with a periodontal probe.
Mucogingival junction (slide 17)?An indistinct line that separates the gingiva from the oral mucosa.
Labels on the gingiva photo (slides 15, 17)?Sulcus, free gingival margin, attached gingiva, mucogingival junction.
Gingiva layers?Deep: fibrous connective tissue; superficial: mucous membrane
Gum colors and meaning?Pink = healthy; pale = anemic; yellow = jaundiced; dark red = inflammation; blue/purple = poor oxygenation
How is gingivitis assessed on probing?Redness, swelling, and bleeding on probing the gingival sulcus
Periodontium — what does 'peri' mean?Around: all tissues around the tooth (gingiva, cementum, periodontal ligament, alveolar bone)

🦴 LO1 · Periodontal ligament & alveolar bone

Periodontal ligament — function (slide 18)?Attaches the root to the alveolar bone.
Clinical application on slide 19 shows…Dog mouth with an exposed/loose canine (periodontal support loss) next to Figure 03-13 — context in transcript.
Alveolar socket (slide 20)?Socket of alveolar bone that houses the tooth.
Interalveolar septa (slide 20)?Bone between ADJACENT teeth.
Interradicular septa (slide 20)?Bone between the ROOTS of the SAME tooth.
Alveolar juga (slide 20)?Bony elevation over the lateral surface of a tooth (root) — visible on the skull.
Lamina dura (slide 20)?Thin dense bone lining the alveolus — radiographically important.
Interalveolar septa in humans = where you…Floss (bone between adjacent teeth)

🧭 LO1 · Surfaces, directions & bite

Four tooth surfaces with subdivisions (slide 21)?1 Occlusal; 2 Contact (A mesial, B distal); 3 Lingual; 4 Vestibular (A labial, B buccal).
Occlusal surface (slide 22)?Contact surface with the tooth of the OPPOSITE jaw.
Contact surface (slide 22)?Surfaces facing adjacent teeth in the SAME dental arch.
Mesial surface (slide 22)?Surface toward the first incisor.
Distal surface (slide 22)?Surface away from the first incisor.
Lingual surface (slide 22)?Faces the tongue — technically the LOWER arcade; the upper arcade's equivalent is the PALATINE surface (lecture only).
Vestibular surface and its two subtypes (slide 22)?Faces the lips or cheeks: labial = facing the lips; buccal = facing the cheeks.
On an incisor vs a molar, which vestibular term applies (slides 21–22)?Incisor/canine (rostral, by lips) = labial; cheek teeth = buccal (yellow vs pink on slide 21).
Coronal direction (slide 23)?Toward the crown.
Apical direction (slide 23)?Toward the apex of the root.
Normal dog bite — superior vs inferior teeth (slide 24)?Superior (upper) teeth bite LATERAL to the inferior (lower) teeth. (Superior/inferior is human terminology — say upper/lower arcade.)
Normal dog bite — canines (slide 24)?Inferior (lower) canine bites ROSTRAL to the superior (upper) canine.
Palatine surface?Upper-arcade surface facing the palate (lingual = lower arcade) — lecture only
Labial vs buccal surfaces — which teeth?Labial = incisors (face the lips); buccal = premolars/molars (face the cheek)

🔢 LO1 · Tooth groups & dental formulae

Heterodont (slides 24–26)?Having different kinds of teeth (incisors, canines, premolars, molars).
Tooth groups in the dog (slide 25)?Incisors, canine, premolars, molars.
Incisor names by number (slide 25)?Incisor 1 = central; Incisor 2 = middle; Incisor 3 = lateral.
Diphyodont (slide 26)?Two sets of teeth develop sequentially: deciduous then permanent.
When is the deciduous set complete / replaced by permanent teeth in the dog (slide 26)?Deciduous complete by 20 days after birth; permanent replace deciduous by 6 months of age (as written on slide).
Carnassial teeth (slide 27)?The largest shearing teeth.
Dog deciduous dental formula and total (slides 26, 28)?2 × (Di3 Dc1 Dp3 / Di3 Dc1 Dp3) = 28.
Dog permanent dental formula and total (slides 26, 28)?2 × (i3 c1 p4 m2 / i3 c1 p4 m3) = 42.
Dog deciduous — missing teeth, molars, carnassials (slide 28)?Upper & lower Dp1 missing; NO molars; carnassials = Dp3/ (upper 3rd deciduous premolar) and Dp/4 (lower 4th deciduous premolar).
Dog adult — missing tooth and carnassials (slide 28)?M3/ (upper 3rd molar) missing; carnassials = p4/ (upper 4th premolar) and m/1 (lower 1st molar).
Cat deciduous dental formula and total (slide 29)?2 × (Di3 Dc1 Dp3 / Di3 Dc1 Dp2) = 26.
Cat permanent dental formula and total (slide 29)?2 × (i3 c1 p3 m1 / i3 c1 p2 m1) = 30.
Cat deciduous — missing teeth, molars, carnassials (slide 29)?Dp1/ missing; Dp/1 and Dp/2 missing; NO molars; carnassials = Dp3/ and Dp/4.
Cat adult — missing teeth and carnassials (slide 29)?p1/ missing; p/1 and p/2 missing; carnassials = p3/ (upper 3rd premolar) and m/1 (lower 1st molar).
Slash notation: what does p4/ vs m/1 mean (slides 28–29)?Slash AFTER the number = UPPER (p4/ = upper 4th premolar); slash BEFORE = LOWER (m/1 = lower 1st molar).
Deciduous vs permanent notation (slides 26–29)?Deciduous = capital 'D' prefix (Di, Dc, Dp); permanent = lowercase i, c, p, m.
Functions of each tooth type?Incisors: nibbling, grooming, social display · canines: puncture/grasp · premolars + molars (cheek teeth): shear/grind
Dental formula reads how?ONE side of the mouth: numerator = upper arcade, denominator = lower arcade; ×2 for the total
Cat deciduous premolar numbering?Upper Dp1 missing → numbered Dp2, Dp3, Dp4; lower Dp1 + Dp2 missing → Dp3, Dp4
Cat adult premolar numbering?Upper p1 missing → p2–p4; lower p1 + p2 missing → p3, p4

🗺️ LO1 · Modified Triadan system

Modified Triadan system — what is it (slides 30–35)?Three-digit tooth numbering: first digit = quadrant, last two digits = tooth position counted from the midline (01 = first incisor).
Permanent quadrant numbers (slide 30)?PATIENT's sides: 100 upper right, 200 upper left, 300 lower left, 400 lower right (clockwise). Facing the dog, its right is on YOUR left.
Deciduous quadrant numbers (slides 34–35)?500 right upper, 600 left upper, 700 left lower, 800 right lower.
Dog permanent Triadan positions for I1–M3 (slide 32)?I1 01, I2 02, I3 03, C 04, P1 05, P2 06, P3 07, P4 08, M1 09, M2 10, M3 11.
Triadan number of the dog's right upper canine (slide 32)?104.
Triadan numbers of the dog's carnassial teeth (slides 28, 32)?Upper 4th premolars 108 / 208; lower 1st molars 309 / 409.
Why do upper arcades end at 110 / 210 in the dog (slide 32)?Upper M3 is absent (shown as '–'); lower arcades run to 311 / 411.
Dog deciduous Triadan — what's missing and how high do numbers go (slide 35)?P1 is 'Missing' in every quadrant (505, 605, 705, 805 absent); numbers go to P4 (508, 608, 708, 808) — no molars.
Clinical application on slide 31 shows…Dr. Diehl's dog Hal: slab fracture of the lower right canine at the pulp → extracted. Triadan number = 404.
Slide 33 figure source?Evans, Anatomy of the Dog 4th ed., fig. 7-6 — dental arcade with Triadan numbers (101–110, 201–210, 301–311, 401–411).
Why does Triadan matter clinically?Dentals: chart present/missing teeth in the medical record and label any teeth extracted
Retained deciduous tooth — how is it numbered?With the deciduous system (500–800); adult teeth still use 100–400

🌱 LO1 · Roots & eruption times

Root count, dog upper arcade I1–M2 (slide 36)?I1–I3: 1; C: 1; P1: 1; P2: 2; P3: 2; P4: 3; M1: 3; M2: 3; (M3 absent).
Root count, dog lower arcade I1–M3 (slide 36)?I1–I3: 1; C: 1; P1: 1; P2–P4: 2; M1: 2; M2: 2; M3: 1.
Which dog teeth have three roots (slide 36)?Upper P4, upper M1, upper M2.
Which dog teeth are single-rooted (slide 36)?All incisors, canines, P1 (upper and lower), and lower M3. Lecture: incisors, canines and the first premolar have one root; the rest of the premolars/molars have 2–3.

👅 LO2 · Pharynx overview

Pharynx — definition (slide 38)?Common cavity through which air and food pass.
What does the pharynx connect (slide 38)?Oral cavity → esophagus; nasal cavity → larynx.
Pharynx compartments (slide 38)?1 Rostral compartment: A nasopharynx (dorsal), B oropharynx (ventral); 2 Caudal compartment (laryngopharynx).
What divides the rostral pharynx into nasopharynx and oropharynx (slides 38, 42)?The soft palate.
Figure 03-27 numbered labels (slides 38–39)?1 nasal cavity; 2 oral cavity; 3 soft palate; 4 nasopharynx; 5 root of tongue; 6 larynx (protruding through pharyngeal floor); 7 laryngopharynx (piriform recess); 8 caudal end of palatopharyngeal arch; 9 esophagus; 10 lamina of cricoid cartilage; 11 trachea.
In Figure 03-27, broken arrow vs single arrow (slide 38)?Broken arrow = passage of food; single arrow = passage of air.
Intrapharyngeal ostium — what meets there and what forms it (slide 39)?Where nasopharynx and oropharynx meet; formed by the soft palate and palatopharyngeal arch.

🚪 LO2 · Pharyngeal boundaries (sagittal section)

Boundary between nasal cavity and nasopharynx (slide 40)?Choanae (openings) at the caudal end of the hard palate.
Boundary between oral cavity and oropharynx (slide 40)?Palatoglossal arch.
What marks the end of the soft palate (slides 40, 42)?Palatopharyngeal arch.
Boundary between laryngopharynx and esophagus (slide 40)?Pharyngoesophageal limen.
Sagittal-section labels, slide 40/45 (numbers 1–12)?1 choanae; 2 nasopharynx; 3 palatoglossal arch; 4 oral cavity; 5 oropharynx; 6 palatopharyngeal arch; 7 soft palate; 8 pharyngoesophageal limen; 9 laryngopharynx; 10 esophagus; 11 epiglottis; 12 other laryngeal cartilages.
Enlarged-pharynx labels, slides 42–43 (numbers 1–11)?1 soft palate; 2 nasopharynx; 3 opening of auditory tube; 4 oropharynx; 5 palatine tonsil; 6 semilunar fold; 7 palatopharyngeal arch; 8 laryngopharynx; 9 epiglottis; 10 root of tongue; 11 bones of floor of cranial cavity.
Order of airway from nose to trachea through the pharynx (slides 38, 46)…Nasal cavity → choanae → nasopharynx → intrapharyngeal ostium → laryngopharynx → larynx → trachea.
Order of food from mouth to esophagus (slides 38, 46)…Oral cavity → palatoglossal arch → oropharynx → (over the epiglottis) laryngopharynx → pharyngoesophageal limen → esophagus.

🍽️ LO2 · Oropharynx & palatine tonsil

Oropharynx extent (slide 41)?Palatoglossal fold (arch) → epiglottis (slide 41 says BASE; lecture said TIP — see flag).
Oropharynx borders (slide 41)?Dorsal: soft palate; ventral: root of the tongue; lateral: tonsillar fossa.
Palatine tonsil — location and covering (slide 41)?In the lateral walls of the oropharynx (tonsillar fossa); covered by the semilunar fold. Lab: find the semilunar fold first, move it aside, then the palatine tonsil is underneath.
Oropharynx communications (slide 41)?Rostrally: oral cavity; caudally: laryngopharynx (over the tip of the epiglottis); dorsally: caudal opening of the nasopharynx.

👃 LO2 · Nasopharynx

Nasopharynx extent and function (slide 43)?Choanae → intrapharyngeal opening; part of the respiratory channel.
Nasopharynx borders (slide 43)?Ventral: soft palate; caudal: epiglottis; lateral: hyopharyngeus and ceratohyoideus muscles (as written — see flag).
Auditory tube openings — location (slide 43)?Inside the nasopharynx (opening of the auditory tube).
Nasopharynx communications (slide 43)?Rostrally: nasal cavity; caudally: laryngopharynx; caudoventrally: oropharynx.

🫁 LO2 · Laryngopharynx

Laryngopharynx extent (slide 44)?Epiglottis → pharyngoesophageal limen.
Laryngopharynx — which systems share it and where it sits (slide 44)?Common segment of the respiratory and digestive systems; located dorsal to the larynx.
Pharyngoesophageal limen — what it is and its level (slide 44)?Annular (ring-like) mucosal boundary between laryngopharynx and esophagus; at the level of the axis (second cervical vertebra — outside knowledge) and cricoid cartilage.
Piriform recesses — location and function (slide 44)?On each side of the base of the epiglottis; continue the oropharyngeal floor around the laryngeal entrance; serve as gutters for fluids.
Laryngopharynx communications (slide 44)?Caudally: esophagus; rostrally: nasopharynx and oropharynx.
Epiglottis during respiration vs swallowing?Breathing: tilts to let air pass into the larynx/trachea · swallowing: tilts to cover the larynx so food goes to the esophagus

🔄 LO2 · Respiration vs swallowing

During respiration, what is the airflow path (slide 46A)?Nasal cavity → nasopharynx (above the soft palate/hard palate) → intrapharyngeal ostium → larynx → trachea.
During swallowing, what is the food path (slide 46B)?Oral cavity → oropharynx (below the soft palate) → laryngopharynx → esophagus.
Landmark labeled at the oral-cavity/oropharynx junction in the swallowing diagram (slide 46)?Palatoglossal fold.
Structures labeled on slide 46 diagrams?Intrapharyngeal ostium, nasopharynx, nasal cavity, soft palate, hard palate, trachea, larynx (A); choana, esophagus, laryngopharynx, soft palate, hard palate, oropharynx, oral cavity, palatoglossal fold (B).

💪 LO2 · Pharyngeal muscles

Rostral pharyngeal constrictors (slide 47)?Pterygopharyngeus and palatopharyngeus.
Pterygopharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: pterygoid bone; insertion: median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Palatopharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: soft palate; insertion: median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Middle pharyngeal constrictor (slide 47)?Hyopharyngeus.
Hyopharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: hyoid bone (thyrohyoid, ceratohyoid); insertion: median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Caudal pharyngeal constrictors (slide 47)?Thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeus.
Thyropharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: thyroid cartilage; insertion: median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Cricopharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: cricoid cartilage; insertion: median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Dilator of the pharynx (slide 47)?Stylopharyngeus.
Stylopharyngeus — origin / insertion (slide 47)?Origin: stylohyoid bone; insertion: lateral pharyngeal wall (NOT the raphe).
Shared insertion of all pharyngeal constrictors (slide 47)?Median dorsal pharyngeal raphe.
Constrictors rostral → caudal, in order (slide 47)…Pterygopharyngeus, palatopharyngeus → hyopharyngeus → thyropharyngeus → cricopharyngeus.
Labels on the dorsal-view pharynx figure (slide 47)?Tongue, oropharynx, soft palate, nasopharynx, thyropharyngeal muscle, 'hypopharyngeal' muscle, cricopharyngeal muscle, median raphe, esophagus, trachea.
