Poole, explained simply · read this first, then the terms page

💡 Air flows when you make a pressure difference, and how easily it flows depends on how stretchy the lung is and how wide the airways are.
🖼️ Picture it: Flow = push ÷ resistance. Like water through a hose: push harder or widen the hose → more flow.

The plain-English version

  • Breathing in: diaphragm contracts → chest bigger → pressure drops → air in. Breathing out at rest: just let go; the lung springs back. Horses are the exception (they actively exhale).
  • Diaphragm = the main breathing muscle. In a galloping horse the rib cage locks and the diaphragm works like a piston. Gut "sloshing" adds only ~5%.
  • Compliance = stretchiness. Recoil = how hard it snaps back.
  • Emphysema = baggy lung: holes in the tissue → too stretchy, weak recoil → airways collapse → hard to get air OUT. Crackles on the stethoscope.
  • Fibrosis = stiff lung: scarring → not stretchy, strong recoil → hard to get air IN. These animals are thin from all the work of breathing.
  • 3 things make the lung stretchy/springy: elastin + collagen (woven like nylon stockings), surface tension, and interdependence (alveoli hold each other open).
  • Surface tension + Laplace: wet bubbles want to shrink, and SMALL bubbles shrink hardest. Surfactant (Type II cells) stops small alveoli from collapsing. Preemies without it can't inflate their lungs.
  • Roaring (horse): paralyzed left larynx flaps in when breathing IN. Honking (toy dogs): weak trachea inside the chest collapses when breathing OUT.
  • Pneumothorax: air in the pleural space → lung shrinks in, chest wall springs out.
  • Most resistance is in the BIG airways. Deep airways are many and in parallel.
  • Blowing out from full lungs: fastest at the start (strong recoil, airways held wide open). Later, flow is set by the lung itself, not by how hard you try ("effort independence").
