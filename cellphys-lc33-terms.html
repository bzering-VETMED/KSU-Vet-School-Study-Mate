🫧 LC33 · Reactive Oxygen & O2 Sensing (Wed 11/11)

Oxygen paradoxS5Oxygen (O2) is essential for almost all life, but organic compounds are flammable, so O2 is also deadly and must be regulated.
Reactive oxygen species (ROS)S6Highly reactive, oxygen-containing compounds; their reactivity makes them very toxic.
Free radicalS6Molecule with an unpaired electron; the most dangerous ROS are often free radicals.
Superoxide anion (O2−·)S6The most basic ROS: single-electron reduction of molecular oxygen (O2 + 1 e− → O2−·). Other ROS form from it.
Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)S6ROS formed spontaneously from superoxide; substrate for catalase, glutathione peroxidase, thioredoxin, and myeloperoxidase.
Hydroxyl radical (HO·)S6Highly reactive free-radical ROS; made by the Fenton reaction, ionizing radiation, and peroxynitrite breakdown.
Hydroperoxyl radical (HOO·)S6Hydroperoxyl radical; listed among ROS (S6) and drawn as a Fenton reaction product (S10).
Nitric oxide radical (·NO)S6Nitrogen-containing reactive species made by nitric oxide synthase; nitrogen species can also be strong ROS.
Nitrogen dioxide radical (·NO2)S6Nitrogen radical; objectives call it the 'nitrite radical'. Forms when peroxynitrite breaks down.
Peroxynitrite (ONOO−)S10Formed when nitric oxide radical reacts with superoxide; breaks down into ·NO2 and HO·.
OxidationS7To take electrons away. ROS are strong oxidizers that remove electrons from other atoms.
Metal center oxidationS7ROS change metal oxidation states: Fe2+ → Fe3+ and Cu+ → Cu2+.
Sulfur (cysteine) oxidationS7Cysteine thiol (–S–H) oxidized to –SO2− then –SO3−, or two thiols joined into a disulfide (–S–S–).
Nitrogen oxidationS7Amine oxidized stepwise: –NH2 → –NHOH → –NHO → –NO2−.
Carbon oxidationS7Methyl oxidized stepwise: –CH3 → –CH2OH → aldehyde → –COOH (carboxylic acid).
SelenocysteineS7Selenium-containing amino acid; 'also important!' (S7). Slides place 'selenocysteines!' at thioredoxin reductase (S15) and glutathione reductase (S16).
Radical chain reactionS8A free-radical ROS takes one electron from a molecule, creating a new radical, which repeats; multiplies damage far beyond the initial molecule.
InitiationS8First step: radical ROS takes an electron from R–H, making a new radical R·.
PropagationS8Each new radical steals an electron from another molecule, making another radical; repeats x times.
TerminationS8Two radicals combine (W· + N· → W–N), ending the chain.
Electron transport chain as ROS sourceS9Normal metabolism: Complexes I and III leak electrons to O2, forming superoxide (slide flags 'LC20!').
Ischemia/reperfusionS9Ischemia: succinate builds up; at reperfusion electrons run backward from Complex II into Complex I, leaking superoxide.
Succinate build-upS9Accumulates at Complex II during ischemia (red arrow fumarate → succinate); drives backward electron flow to Complex I and superoxide on reperfusion.
Phagocytic respiratory burstS10Phagocytes deliberately make ROS in the phagosome: NADPH oxidase → superoxide → H2O2 → hypochlorous acid.
NADPH oxidaseS10Enzyme: NADPH + 2 O2 → NADP+ + H+ + 2 O2−·. Starts the respiratory burst; also an O2 sensor in glomus cells.
Superoxide dismutase (SOD)S14Enzyme: 2 O2−· + 2 H+ → H2O2 + O2. Found in virtually every cell; also part of the respiratory burst.
MyeloperoxidaseS10Phagocyte enzyme: H2O2 + HCl → HOCl (bleach) + H2O.
Hypochlorous acid (HOCl)S10'Bleach'; the killing product of myeloperoxidase in the phagosome.
Fenton reactionS10Iron-catalyzed (slide header Fe2+; figure Fe^x+): 2 H2O2 → H2O + HOO· + HO·. Iron intensifies ROS.
Nitric oxide synthase (NOS)S10Enzyme: arginine + NADPH + H+ + O2 → citrulline + H2O + NADP+ + nitric oxide radical (·NO).
Ionizing radiationS10Alpha, beta, gamma radiation splits water (H–O–H → HO· + e− + H+); the electron reduces O2 to superoxide.
ROS damage to DNAS11Oxidized DNA, cross-linked DNA, DNA adducts.
ROS damage to lipidsS11Lipid peroxides and lipid adducts; multiplied by radical chain reactions.
ROS damage to proteinsS11Oxidized amino acids, protein dimers, protein adducts.
ROS outcomesS11Cell damage → cell death, neoplasm, other diseases, and aging (slide puts '?' after aging).
Acetaminophen (Tylenol) toxicityS12Cat case diagnosis (S3 signs: rapid breathing, nausea, drooling, lethargy, cyanosis, edema).
GlucuronidationS12Primary non-toxic acetaminophen pathway. Cats lack the gene for it.
Hydroxylation (acetaminophen)S12Minor side pathway that produces NAPQI, a strong oxidizer.
NAPQIS12N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (expansion OUTSIDE). Strong oxidizer from acetaminophen; causes massive oxidative liver damage and cell death.
N-acetylcysteineS19Treatment for acetaminophen toxicity: precursor for glutathione production, restoring reduced glutathione stores.
Enzymatic antioxidantsS13Superoxide dismutase and catalase.
CatalaseS14Enzyme: 2 H2O2 → 2 H2O + O2. With superoxide dismutase prevents superoxide/H2O2 build-up.
Protein antioxidantsS13Thioredoxin.
ThioredoxinS15Protein with two cysteine thiols; reduced form (S–H H–S) reduces H2O2 to water and becomes oxidized (S–S).
Thioredoxin reductase (TrxR)S15Regenerates reduced thioredoxin using NADPH; contains selenocysteines. TrxR inhibitors are possible anti-cancer drugs.
NADPHS15Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (reduced): electron donor that powers thioredoxin and glutathione regeneration cycles.
Peptide antioxidantsS13Glutathione.
Glutathione (GSH)S16Tripeptide glutamate–cysteine–glycine; reduced form neutralizes H2O2 and detoxifies NAPQI.
Glutathione disulfide (GS–SG)S16Oxidized glutathione: two GSH joined by a disulfide after reducing H2O2.
Glutathione peroxidaseS16Enzyme: H2O2 + 2 GSH → GS–SG + 2 H2O.
Glutathione reductaseS16Enzyme: regenerates 2 GSH from GS–SG using NADPH; slide labels it 'selenocysteines!' (see flag).
Small molecule antioxidantsS13Vitamin C and vitamin E.
Vitamin C (ascorbate)S17Donates an H+ and e− to a radical (R· → R–H), becoming a relatively stable radical, then dehydroascorbate.
Dehydroascorbate (DHAsc)S17Fully oxidized vitamin C: 2 R· + 2 Asc → 2 R–H + Asc + DHAsc.
Vitamin ES18Donates H+ and e− to a radical, becoming a very stable radical; regenerated by vitamin C.
TransferrinS13OUTSIDE — confirm: plasma protein that carries Fe3+, keeping free iron (Fenton catalyst) low. Listed in terms, not shown.
Transferrin receptorS13OUTSIDE — confirm: cell-surface receptor that takes up iron-loaded transferrin by endocytosis. Listed in terms, not shown.
FerritinS13OUTSIDE — confirm: intracellular iron-storage protein that sequesters iron safely. Listed in terms, not shown.
Normoxia / hypoxia / hyperoxiaS20Normal / low / high oxygen levels; each triggers different signaling responses.
Central receptorsS22Ventrolateral surface of the medulla; stimulate breathing but sense primarily CO2 (as pH), not O2.
Peripheral receptorsS22Carotid and aortic bodies; respond to pO2 as well as pCO2 and pH; sense O2 through glomus cells.
Carotid bodiesS21Peripheral O2 receptor site (carotid artery).
Aortic bodiesS21Peripheral O2 receptor site (aortic arch).
Glomus cellS23Peripheral O2-sensing cell: hypoxia closes K+ channels → depolarizes → Ca2+ channels open → neurotransmitter release.
Heme oxygenaseS23Glomus O2 sensor: uses O2 + NADPH to make carbon monoxide (CO) during normoxia; CO's link to the channel is not drawn.
AMP kinase (AMPK)S23Adenosine monophosphate–activated kinase; glomus sensor: hypoxia drops ATP, raises AMP → AMP kinase inhibits the K+ channel.
NADPH oxidase (O2 sensor)S23In glomus cells, makes superoxide from O2 during normoxia; superoxide signals to the K+ channel (keeps it open).
Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIF)S24Transcription factors that sense low O2 in individual cells and turn on hypoxia response genes.
HIF prolyl hydroxylaseS24Uses O2 to add –OH to HIF in normoxia, targeting HIF for proteasomal degradation.
Proteasomal degradationS24Fate of hydroxylated HIF when O2 is high.
Hypoxia response genesS24Genes HIF turns on when O2 is low and HIF escapes degradation.
ASK1S25Apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1: a MAPKKK held by reduced thioredoxin; released in hyperoxia/oxidative stress.
MAPKKK → MAPKK → MAPKS25Mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade: ASK1 (MAPKKK) activates MAPKK, then MAPK → pro-apoptotic gene expression.
Pro-apoptotic gene expressionS25Hyperoxia response output of the ASK1–MAPK pathway.
